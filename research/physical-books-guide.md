# Physical Books Guide for Madrid Toponymy Research

Practical guide for finding and consulting physical books at the **Biblioteca Tomas Navarro Tomas** (CCHS-CSIC, Albasanz 26-28) and other CSIC libraries. Prioritized by usefulness for this project.

## Strategy

Most of these books are **alphabetical dictionaries or encyclopedias** — you do NOT need to read them cover to cover. The efficient approach:

1. **Targeted lookups**: Bring a list of the ~40 station names that still have `unknown` or `uncertain` etymology. Look up each name in the alphabetical index.
2. **Photograph the relevant entries**: A phone camera is enough. Each entry is typically 1/4 to 1 full page.
3. **Check the introduction**: Many authors explain their methodology and linguistic conventions in the first 20-30 pages. Worth skimming once.

Estimated total effort: **2-3 focused sessions of ~1 hour each**, not hundreds of pages of photography.

## Priority 1: Definitive Sources (check these first)

### 1. "Metro de Madrid: Por que sus estaciones se llaman asi?" — Jose Felipe Alonso Fernandez-Checa (2023)

- **Pages**: 276 pp
- **Organization**: By metro line (Line 1, Line 2, ... Line 12, Ramal), then station by station
- **Publisher**: Self-published / Amazon
- **ISBN**: 978-84-09-54335-1
- **Why essential**: THE single definitive source for every metro station name origin. Covers all 302+ stations. This one book could fill virtually every remaining gap in our metro CSV.
- **What to look up**: Every station still marked `uncertain` or `unknown`. Also cross-check stations where we only have web sources — this book likely has deeper historical context.
- **Practical approach**: The book is organized by line, so flip to the relevant line chapter and photograph entries for target stations. ~40 entries = ~40 photos.
- **Availability**: Likely available at Biblioteca Nacional (BNE). Check CSIC OPAC and BNE catalogue. Also available on Amazon (~15-20 EUR) if purchase is preferred — at 276pp it's a quick reference book.

### 2. "Toponimia madrilena: proceso evolutivo" — Luis Miguel Aparisi Laporta (2001)

- **Pages**: Vol. 1 ~760pp, Vol. 2 ~470pp, + CD-ROM with full nomenclator
- **Organization**: Vol. 1 = historical essay on Madrid's naming evolution. Vol. 2 = alphabetical nomenclator of 20,000+ place names with origins.
- **Publisher**: Ayuntamiento de Madrid (Gerencia Municipal de Urbanismo)
- **ISBN**: 84-7812-524-7
- **Why essential**: The most comprehensive Madrid-specific toponymy reference ever compiled. Covers streets, plazas, barrios, districts, and geographic features. The CD-ROM contains a searchable database.
- **What to look up**: Focus on Vol. 2 (the alphabetical nomenclator). Look up every neighbourhood, district, and street name in our dataset. The entries typically give: first documented mention, etymology, historical context.
- **Practical approach**: Vol. 2 is the priority — it's an alphabetical dictionary. Look up ~60-80 target names, photograph each entry. The CD-ROM would be ideal if a computer with a CD drive is available.
- **Availability**: Published by Ayuntamiento de Madrid. Should be at Biblioteca Tomas Navarro Tomas or Biblioteca Nacional. Also sometimes found at Biblioteca Regional de Madrid (Joaquin Leguina).

### 3. "Los nombres de las calles de Madrid" — Maria Isabel Gea Ortigas (various editions, 1993-2020)

- **Pages**: ~324 pp (varies by edition)
- **Organization**: Alphabetical dictionary of street names
- **Publisher**: La Libreria (Madrid)
- **ISBN**: 978-84-87290-94-2 (2009 edition)
- **Why essential**: The standard popular reference for Madrid street name origins. Covers 1,000+ streets. Multiple editions mean it has been updated and corrected over decades.
- **What to look up**: All 30 streets in our streets CSV, plus any street names that appear as station names (many metro stations are named after streets).
- **Practical approach**: Pure alphabetical lookup. ~50 target names = ~50 photos.
- **Availability**: Very widely held. Biblioteca TNT, BNE, Biblioteca Regional, most Madrid public libraries. Also still in print and sold at La Libreria bookshop (Calle Mayor 80).

## Priority 2: Scholarly Linguistic References

### 4. "Toponimia prerromana hispana" — Emilio Nieto Ballester (1997)

- **Pages**: 448 pp
- **Organization**: Alphabetical dictionary of ~2,000 pre-Roman, Celtic, Iberian, Basque, and Latin-substrate toponyms across all of Spain
- **Publisher**: Gredos (Biblioteca Romanica Hispanica)
- **ISBN**: 84-249-1836-0
- **Why essential**: The scholarly standard for pre-Roman and early-Latin etymologies. Critical for names like Leganes, Getafe, Mostoles, Coslada, Alcorcon, Fuenlabrada — the oldest and most disputed Madrid toponyms.
- **What to look up**: All Cercanias/metro stations with pre-Roman or uncertain ancient etymologies (~15-20 names).
- **Practical approach**: Alphabetical, so direct lookup. Each entry is typically 1/2 to 1 page with full linguistic discussion.
- **Availability**: Gredos is a major publisher. Should be at Biblioteca TNT (Linguistics/Philology section). Also at BNE.

### 5. "Atlas toponimico de Espana" — Jairo Javier Garcia Sanchez (2007)

- **Pages**: 408 pp
- **Organization**: By linguistic stratum — pre-Roman (chapters on Celtic, Basque, Iberian), Latin, Germanic, Arabic, Romance. Within each chapter, toponyms are discussed thematically.
- **Publisher**: Arco/Libros
- **ISBN**: 978-84-7635-667-4
- **Why essential**: Modern scholarly synthesis of Spanish toponymy organized by language layer. Garcia Sanchez is one of the leading living authorities. His CVC Rinconete articles (which we already have) are drawn from this book.
- **What to look up**: Use the index (indice de toponimos) at the back to find Madrid-area names. The thematic organization means you may need to check multiple chapters for a single name.
- **Practical approach**: Start with the index. Photograph the pages referenced for each Madrid toponym. Probably 20-30 relevant pages scattered across the book.
- **Availability**: Arco/Libros is a standard academic publisher. Biblioteca TNT should have it.

### 6. "Origenes del espanol" (10th ed.) — Ramon Menendez Pidal (2005, orig. 1926)

- **Pages**: ~316 pp (10th edition with notes by Diego Catalan)
- **Organization**: Collected essays on the origins of Castilian, including chapters on toponymic evidence for sound changes
- **Publisher**: Espasa-Calpe / Real Academia Espanola
- **ISBN**: 978-84-670-1854-3
- **Why essential**: The foundational work on historical Spanish linguistics. Menendez Pidal's etymological analyses are still cited as authoritative. Particularly important for understanding the phonetic evolution of Madrid-area toponyms.
- **What to look up**: Check the index for specific place names (Madrit, Magerit, etc.). The chapters on Arabic-Romance contact and sound changes are most relevant.
- **Practical approach**: This is more of a reference read than a lookup book. Skim the index, photograph relevant passages. ~10-15 pages likely relevant.
- **Availability**: A classic — every Spanish linguistics library has multiple copies. Definitely at Biblioteca TNT.

## Priority 3: Arabic-Stratum Specialists

### 7. "Historia del nombre 'Madrid'" — Jaime Oliver Asin (1991, orig. 1959)

- **Pages**: 412 pp + plates
- **Organization**: Monograph dedicated entirely to the etymology of "Madrid". Detailed linguistic analysis of Magerit/Matrit/Mayrit theories.
- **Publisher**: AECI (Agencia Espanola de Cooperacion Internacional)
- **ISBN**: 84-7232-567-3
- **Why essential**: The most thorough analysis ever written on the origin of Madrid's own name. Proposes the Arabic *mayra* (channel/conduit) theory. Even if focused on one name, the methodology and historical context illuminate many other Madrid-area Arabic-origin toponyms.
- **What to look up**: The core argument (chapters 2-5). Also check if appendices discuss other local toponyms.
- **Practical approach**: This is a focused monograph — worth reading the core argument (~50-80 pages) rather than just photographing entries. Take notes on the Arabic etymological methodology.
- **Availability**: May be harder to find — the 1991 reprint had limited circulation. Check CSIC OPAC. BNE definitely has it. The 1959 original may be in the rare books section.

### 8. "Contribucion a la toponomastica arabe de Espana" — Miguel Asin Palacios (1944)

- **Pages**: 156 pp
- **Organization**: Introduction on Arabic toponymic methodology (~30 pp) + alphabetical glossary of Arabic-origin Spanish place names (~120 pp)
- **Publisher**: CSIC (Instituto Benito Arias Montano)
- **ISBN**: N/A (1944 publication)
- **Why essential**: The pioneer work on Arabic-origin toponymy in Spain, by a legendary Arabist. Many of his etymologies are still cited. Relevant for: Alcorcon, Alcobendas, Alcala, Aluche, Chamartin, and any toponym beginning with Al-/Guad-/Ben-.
- **What to look up**: The alphabetical glossary — look up every Arabic-origin station name (~15-20 names).
- **Practical approach**: At 156 pages total, the glossary section is quite compact. Could photograph the entire glossary in 10 minutes.
- **Availability**: Published by CSIC itself — Biblioteca TNT almost certainly has it. May also be on Digital CSIC. Check the digital catalogue first.

### 9. "Los toponomios: sus blazones y trofeos" — Alvaro Galmes de Fuentes (2000)

- **Pages**: 211 pp
- **Organization**: Essay format. Chapters organized by theme (rivers, mountains, cities) with etymological discussions woven in.
- **Publisher**: Real Academia de la Historia
- **ISBN**: 84-89512-73-5
- **Why essential**: Galmes de Fuentes was a leading Romanist who specialized in Arabic-Romance linguistic contact. His discussions of Mozarabic and Arabic toponyms are authoritative.
- **What to look up**: Use the index to find Madrid-area references. The chapters on city names and Arabic contributions are most relevant.
- **Practical approach**: Check index, photograph relevant passages. ~10-15 pages likely.
- **Availability**: Published by Real Academia de la Historia. Should be at major research libraries. Check CSIC OPAC.

## Priority 4: Street-Level References

### 10. "Las calles de Madrid" — Pedro de Repide (2011 reprint, orig. 1920s)

- **Pages**: ~805 pp
- **Organization**: Alphabetical dictionary of Madrid streets. Each entry includes: location, name origin, historical anecdotes, notable residents.
- **Publisher**: La Libreria (Madrid) — modern reprint; original published as newspaper serial in La Libertad (1921-1925)
- **ISBN**: 978-84-87290-98-0
- **Why essential**: A Madrid classic and literary monument. Repide was a journalist and cronista de la Villa. His entries are rich in historical color, though etymological rigor varies (he wrote before modern linguistic methods).
- **What to look up**: Streets that appear as metro/Cercanias station names. Cross-reference with Gea Ortigas for more modern analysis.
- **Practical approach**: Alphabetical lookup. The entries are longer (often 1-2 pages each with anecdotes), so this is also enjoyable to browse.
- **Availability**: Widely reprinted. Biblioteca TNT, BNE, Biblioteca Regional. Still in print at La Libreria.

### 11. "Toponimia y arqueologia del Madrid medieval" — Catalina Santa Cecilia & Enrique Fernandez Arroyo (2006)

- **Pages**: Unknown (possibly ~200-300 pp based on similar publications)
- **Organization**: Organized by categories of natural referents (water features, terrain, vegetation, fauna) and their reflection in medieval Madrid place names
- **Publisher**: Unknown / possibly institutional
- **Why essential**: Specifically links medieval archaeological evidence to toponymic data for Madrid. Useful for understanding the oldest layer of neighbourhood and district names (Atocha, Vistillas, Carabanchel, etc.).
- **What to look up**: The chapters on water-related and terrain-related toponyms. Many station names derive from medieval geographic features.
- **Practical approach**: This may be harder to find. Check CSIC OPAC and Dialnet for bibliographic details first.
- **Availability**: May be a conference proceedings volume or institutional publication. Check CSIC catalogue.

## Copyright & Digitization Guide

**Question**: Can you digitize and publicly share information from these books in your CC-BY-SA 4.0 dataset?

### What you CAN safely do (no copyright issues):

1. **Extract factual data** (etymologies, dates, linguistic derivations, historical references) and express them **in your own words**. Facts are not copyrightable -- only the specific creative expression is. Writing "García Sánchez (2007) proposes the Arabic 'jata' theory for Getafe" is perfectly fine.

2. **Short quotations** with proper attribution. Spanish copyright law (Ley de Propiedad Intelectual, Art. 32) allows citation for research/criticism purposes as long as the source is identified and the quote is proportional. A sentence or two from a 276-page book is well within fair use.

3. **Bibliographic references**. Listing "Alonso Fernández-Checa, J.F. (2023). Metro de Madrid: ¿Por qué sus estaciones se llaman así? Madrid: La Librería. ISBN 978-84-09-54335-1" is not only legal but expected in scholarly work.

4. **Paraphrase and synthesize** across multiple sources. Your etymology summaries that combine findings from 3-4 sources in your own words are completely fine.

5. **Government/institutional publications**: The Felipe II Relaciones Topográficas, the Fuero de Madrid, Catastro de Ensenada, and other historical crown documents are public domain. The CVC Cervantes articles are published with open access by a public institution.

### What you should AVOID:

1. **Reproducing entire entries verbatim** from books like Aparisi Laporta or Gea Ortigas. Even a single full dictionary entry (e.g., the complete text of the "Pitis" entry if it exists) could be seen as reproducing the creative arrangement and expression.

2. **Scanning/photographing and publishing entire pages** or substantial portions of in-copyright books. Use photos only for your personal research notes.

3. **Reproducing the structure/organization** of a book as your own. If Alonso Fernández-Checa's book organizes stations by line with a specific narrative approach, don't copy that structure.

### The safe approach for your dataset:

Your current approach is already the right one: write **original etymology summaries** in your own words that synthesize multiple sources, cite all sources in the `source` field, and use `confidence` levels. This is standard scholarly practice and creates no copyright issues.

When you consult the physical books:
1. Take notes (not verbatim copies)
2. Write the etymology summary in your own words
3. Cite the book as a source: "Alonso Fernández-Checa 2023"
4. If a specific quote is illuminating, keep it short and in quotation marks

**Bottom line**: You will NOT get in trouble if you extract facts, paraphrase, and cite properly. You WOULD get in trouble if you scanned and published entire chapters or entries verbatim. Your dataset's CC-BY-SA license is compatible with this approach because you're creating an original derivative work, not reproducing copyrighted text.

## Tips for the Biblioteca Tomas Navarro Tomas

- The library specializes in **Humanities and Social Sciences** — all the linguistics and history books above fall within its collection scope.
- Check the **CSIC OPAC** (aleph.csic.es) before going downstairs to confirm shelf location and availability.
- The **CD-ROM from Aparisi Laporta** may be in a special media section — ask the librarian.
- Some older works (Asin Palacios 1944, Oliver Asin 1959) may be in the **deposito** (closed stacks) — request them in advance if possible.
- The library allows **in-situ photography** for personal research use — confirm current policy at the desk.
- If a book is not at TNT, use **CSIC interlibrary loan** (prestamo interbibliotecario) to request it from other CSIC libraries.

Moncloa station is named after its immediate urban setting: the Moncloa area at the northwest edge of central Madrid, beside Plaza de Moncloa, Calle de la Princesa, the Ciudad Universitaria access, and today’s major bus interchange. The name was already a Madrid place-name long before the Metro station opened.

The best-supported origin is not the Metro itself but a historic estate. A modern government guide to the Moncloa complex explains that, in 1660, Gaspar de Haro y Guzmán acquired two horticultural properties: the huerta de La Monclova and the huerta de Sora. Their names came from earlier owners, respectively the Count of La Monclova and the Count of Sora. A republication of Joaquín Ezquerra del Bayo’s study gives an even clearer clue: the Madrid property had previously been known as Fuente el Sol, and its Moncloa name was due to its possession by the Count of La Monclova and his children until 1660.

That makes the usual deeper story more delicate. Some popular accounts explain Monclova from Latin forms such as 'Mons Clovis', tied to the Sevillian place and castle of La Monclova near Fuentes de Andalucía. However, the authoritative Madrid sources prove the Madrid transmission through the noble title and estate, while they do not independently prove the Latin etymology of the Sevillian name. For public wording, the safer wording is that Madrid’s Moncloa derives from Monclova through the Counts of La Monclova; the older Latin explanation should be treated as unverified unless a specialist toponymic source is added.

The transport chronology is also important. Moncloa entered the Metro network on 17 July 1963, when Line 3 was extended from Argüelles to Moncloa. The Line 6 platforms arrived much later, on 10 May 1995, as part of the Laguna-Ciudad Universitaria opening that completed the Circular line. In 2006, the Line 3 station was closed and then reopened in a new alignment so the interchange could be expanded and the Line 3 platforms placed more conveniently with Line 6.

The surrounding name has acquired political and urban meanings beyond the station. The rebuilt Palacio de la Moncloa, constructed after the Civil War destruction of the old site, became the official residence and seat of the Spanish prime minister in 1977. Nearby, the Arco de la Victoria marks another layer of Moncloa’s twentieth-century history, but it is context rather than the source of the station name.

Humanes station takes its name from Humanes de Madrid, the municipality in which it is located. Today it is the southern terminus of Madrid Cercanias line C-5 for many services, and official transport sources list the station simply as Humanes, with access from Calle Cenicientos and municipal location in Humanes de Madrid.

Behind the station name is a much older place-name. Humanes appears in medieval documentation in 1141, when Alfonso VII donated the villa to Pedro Brimonis or Pedro Brimon. The document describes a settlement between Madrid and Toledo, which helps explain why the place was later distinguished as Humanes de Toledo or Humanes de Madrid in different contexts.

The origin of the word Humanes is not fully settled. A widely repeated modern hypothesis, attributed to linguist Edelmiro Bascuas, relates Humanes to a Paleo-European hydronymic base connected with channels or watercourses. An consulted article by Fernando Jimenez de Gregorio in Anales del Instituto de Estudios Madrilenos instead derives the name from Latin humidus, 'humid', and points to the local landscape: arroyos, wet places, wells, ponds and names such as Las Arroyadas, El Charco and La Laguna.

That water reading fits the geography better than it may first appear. Humanes de Madrid lies on a mostly flat southern Madrid landscape, historically agricultural and crossed by small watercourses such as Valdehondillo del Prado and Las Arroyadas. The broader Guaten/Humanejos stream system is also tied to this area, reinforcing why older residents and scholars associated the toponym with damp ground rather than with a person or a modern urban feature.

For the railway, the name is older than the current Cercanias service. Humanes had a railway presence on the Madrid-Caceres-Portugal route in the nineteenth century, while the C-5 extension to Humanes entered service on 28 February 2004. The safest interpretation is therefore two-layered: the station is named for the municipality, and the municipality's name is probably a water-related toponym, with the Latin humidus explanation better documented in consulted Madrid-specific sources and the Paleo-European hydronymic theory remaining a plausible but less directly verified alternative.

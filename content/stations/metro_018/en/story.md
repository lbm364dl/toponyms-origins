Príncipe Pío station is named for the place around it: the old Príncipe Pío estate and the nearby Montaña del Príncipe Pío. The current transport name replaced the older Norte name in December 1994, when the station was being rebuilt as a Metro, Cercanías and bus interchange. In etymological terms, the name reaches the station through the landscape, but the landscape name itself comes from a person.

That person was Francisco Pío de Saboya y Moura, usually dated 1672-1723 in the stronger biographical record. He was an Italian-born aristocrat in Spanish service, Prince of San Gregorio and Marquess of Castel-Rodrigo, and a military commander aligned with Felipe V during and after the War of the Spanish Succession. The historic possession associated with his house lay in this western edge of Madrid, near the Manzanares and La Florida.

Before the modern interchange, the place was dominated by railway history. The Estación del Norte took its name not from geography alone but from the Compañía de los Ferrocarriles del Norte, which built Madrid's terminal for the line toward the French border. A primitive station operated from June 1861; the present historic building opened on 8 July 1882. By the late twentieth century, the long-distance terminal role had faded, and the area was recast as a metropolitan interchange.

The name also carries a powerful urban memory beyond the aristocratic estate. The Montaña del Príncipe Pío was one of the sites where French troops executed prisoners after the uprising of 2 May 1808. Madrid's municipal heritage record for the Florida cemetery identifies forty-three victims buried there, and the Prado preserves Goya's great painting known as *El 3 de mayo de 1808 en Madrid* or the shootings on the Montaña del Príncipe Pío.

The station name is therefore best explained as a historic place name of personal origin. It is safe to connect Príncipe Pío with Francisco Pío de Saboya y Moura and with the old estate, but less safe to say that the 1994 renaming was officially intended to honor the whole sequence of aristocratic, railway and anti-Napoleonic memory. The most precise account is that Norte became Príncipe Pío as the station adopted the established local toponym during its conversion into a modern interchange.

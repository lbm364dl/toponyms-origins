The name of Universidad P. Comillas station is direct and practical. It identifies the Cercanías stop serving the Cantoblanco campus of Universidad Pontificia Comillas, on the northern edge of Madrid. Adif uses the fuller station name Universidad Pontificia de Comillas, while university guidance for students also uses the shorter travel form Universidad P. Comillas.

The university name comes from Comillas, the Cantabrian town where the institution was born. On 16 December 1890, Pope Leo XIII erected a pontifical seminary there by the brief Sempiternam Dominici Gregis. The project was tied to the first and second Marquesses of Comillas, Antonio López y López and Claudio López Bru, and the direction of the seminary was entrusted to the Society of Jesus.

In 1904, Pope Pius X authorized the Seminario Pontificio de Comillas to confer academic degrees in Philosophy, Theology, and Canon Law. During the 1960s the Holy See encouraged a move from the Cantabrian town to Madrid, and the transfer was authorized by a letter of Pope Paul VI dated 24 January 1969. The university describes that move as gradual, with the Madrid stage becoming central to its modern identity.

The railway stop belongs to a later transport story. The regional rail history published by the Consorcio Regional de Transportes records the 2001 opening of the Cantoblanco-Alcobendas-San Sebastián de los Reyes branch and a new open-air stop built beside Universidad de Comillas. Comillas' own access information tells students bound for Cantoblanco to use the C-4 toward Alcobendas-San Sebastián de los Reyes and get off at Universidad Pontificia Comillas, not at Universidad-Cantoblanco.

The older place-name Comillas is more complex than the station name itself. Popular explanations sometimes play with 'cinco millas', but the medieval forms Cumiyas and Cumillas make that story unlikely. A stronger toponymic explanation connects the name with curved terrain, small valleys, or rounded hills; the dataset's Latin cumella 'little hilltop' wording should therefore be treated as an oversimplified or weak form of that broader landscape-based interpretation.

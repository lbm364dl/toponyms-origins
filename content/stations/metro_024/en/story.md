The name of Concha Espina station is best understood through Madrid's street geography. The station serves Line 9 in Chamartín, with access on Calle del Príncipe de Vergara near Plaza de Cataluña, and it sits by the urban axis associated with Avenida de Concha Espina. The official Madrid street directory lists that avenue in Chamartín, running from Paseo de la Castellana to Calle del Príncipe de Vergara.

The person behind the avenue was Concha Espina, the literary name of Concepción Espina y Tagle. The Real Academia de la Historia identifies her as a writer born in Santander on 15 April 1869 and dead in Madrid on 19 May 1955. Her career moved between poetry, journalism, fiction and public literary life, but her fame came especially from narrative prose.

Her early life also explains why her name travelled beyond Madrid. After family financial difficulties, time in Mazcuerras and a period in Chile, Espina built a professional writing career in Spain and across the Hispanic press world. The Biblioteca Nacional de España highlights La niña de Luzmela and La esfinge maragata among the works that established her, and notes that La esfinge maragata made her the first woman to win the Real Academia Española's Fastenrath Prize.

One striking cultural detail is that her fiction helped rename a landscape. The town of Mazcuerras, where she lived, is also known as Luzmela because of La niña de Luzmela, and the local council still presents Concha Espina's house and the Luzmela association as part of its municipal heritage. In Madrid, her name belongs to a major avenue close to the Santiago Bernabéu area and, through that avenue, to the Metro station.

The station name is therefore well supported as a personal commemorative name, although the station probably received it indirectly from the avenue rather than from a separate biographical naming act. The station opened on 30 December 1983, when the Plaza de Castilla-Avenida de América section entered service. Claims about her Nobel history should be stated carefully: the official Nobel archive records 25 nominations in Literature, concentrated in 1926-1932 and then again in 1952 and 1954, but she never received the prize.

Pitis is a place-name station. Metro did not coin the word: when line 7 reached this point on 29 March 1999, it was explicitly linking the Metro network with the existing Cercanías station of Pitis. Renfe and the regional transport consortium still describe it as the interchange between Metro line 7 and the Cercanías services at the northwestern edge of Madrid.

The older name belongs to the rural landscape of former Fuencarral, today in the Fuencarral-El Pardo district. Madrid City Council land inventories preserve several nearby site descriptions with the old wording of rural property records: Pitis, Vereda de Córdoba or Huertas de Pitis, and Hoya del Rencajo o Pitis. That kind of wording is important because it shows Pitis functioning as a local paraje, not simply as a late twentieth-century station label.

Before the PAU of Arroyo del Fresno and the modern streets around Gloria Fuertes and María Casares, this edge of Madrid was remembered as open land. Local historical writing about Peñagrande and Lacoma describes Pitis around the 1960s as a leafy poplar grove with a small stream where neighbors went to spend time outdoors. That memory fits the station's position near Arroyo del Fresno, the Monte de El Pardo approaches and a zone that long sat between city, railway and countryside.

The transport chronology explains why the old place-name became so visible. The railway station existed before the Metro extension; later, the Comunidad de Madrid's line 7 works rehabilitated the old station building and created an interchange. The same name then became a Metro terminus, a Cercanías stop and a bus reference point, turning a minor rural toponym into a familiar name on Madrid transport maps.

The origin of the word Pitis itself remains uncertain. A Madrid toponymy blog listed it among natural place-names but did not provide a derivation, and no stronger academic or official etymology was found. A possible link with Greek Pitys, 'pine', is too speculative for public wording as an origin: there is no demonstrated route by which that Greek form would have entered local Castilian rural toponymy here. The safest account is therefore modest: Pitis is an old local place-name, documented around the station area, whose deeper linguistic source is still unknown.

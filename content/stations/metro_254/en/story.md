Ciudad de los Ángeles station is named after the residential area it serves in Villaverde: Ciudad de los Ángeles, the popular name for part of the official Los Ángeles barrio. The name is therefore a place-name first, not a direct dedication to a person or an event. When Line 3 was extended south to Villaverde Alto, the station kept the neighbourhood name already used by residents, schools, parks and local streets.

The older reference behind the neighbourhood name is the Cerro de los Ángeles in nearby Getafe. Local tradition says the name Ciudad de los Ángeles was selected in 1958 by popular vote because the new settlement lay close to that famous hill. The hill itself takes its name from Nuestra Señora de los Ángeles, venerated in Getafe, and is marked by the hermitage and the great religious complex of the Sagrado Corazón.

The urban story is also essential. Ciudad de los Ángeles was planned and built mainly between the 1950s and 1960s as a satellite city for a growing working population, including migrants arriving in Madrid from other parts of Spain. Architectural sources connect the project with Secundino de Zuazo, Javier de Zuazo and Manuel Muñoz Monasterio, and describe it as a large residential operation beside the Carretera or Avenida de Andalucía, close to major industrial employment such as the Barreiros factory.

The neighbourhood has a distinctive cultural map. Many of its streets are named after zarzuelas, including titles such as La Verbena de la Paloma, Pan y Toros, La del Manojo de Rosas and La Alegría de la Huerta. Local sources state that this street-naming pattern was adopted in 1983, after a period when addresses were often identified by block numbers rather than ordinary street names.

The strongest conclusion is that the station is named after the neighbourhood, and that the neighbourhood name is very plausibly tied to the Cerro de los Ángeles. The weaker part is the exact documentation of the 1958 popular contest, which is repeated in local accounts but still needs a primary archival source. The Cerro's status as the geographic centre of the Iberian Peninsula should be worded carefully: official and tourism sources describe it as traditionally or commonly considered central, while other calculations and local claims place the centre elsewhere.

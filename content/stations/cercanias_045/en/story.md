Guadalajara station is named in the most direct way: after Guadalajara, the city and provincial capital it serves. In the Madrid Cercanias network, the Consorcio Regional de Transportes de Madrid lists Guadalajara as a Cercanias station with C-2 and C-8 correspondences, and Adif identifies the railway station simply as Guadalajara at Calle Francisco Aritio, s/n.

Behind that modern railway label is a much older place name. Guadalajara City Council's tourism history says the early settlement was known in the Islamic period by two names: Madinat al-Faray and Wad al-Hayara. The same municipal source explains Wad al-Hayara as an Arabic translation of the pre-Roman name Arriaca, a name traditionally connected with stones, stony ground, or a stony watercourse.

The usual public explanation, therefore, is broadly right but should be worded carefully. Guadalajara is often glossed as 'river of stones' or 'valley of stones', from Arabic wadi plus a word for stones. However, the strongest local public source does not present it only as a fresh description of the gravelly Henares; it frames the Arabic name as a translation or adaptation of an older toponym, Arriaca.

The city's medieval turning point came in 1085, when Guadalajara entered the kingdom of Castile in the expansion of Alfonso VI. Later tradition turned that episode into the story of a military feat by Alvar Fanez de Minaya, a figure still reflected in the city's civic imagery. The Mendoza family later gave Guadalajara one of its most visible monuments, the Palacio del Infantado, begun around 1480 and now home to the Museum of Guadalajara after major twentieth-century destruction and restoration.

For the station name itself, there is no serious competing explanation: it is the city name. The uncertainty lies in the deeper etymology of the city name, especially how securely Arriaca should be linked to the present city and whether the best English gloss should be 'river', 'valley', or more generally a stony wadi or watercourse. A cautious display should say that Guadalajara is traditionally explained from Arabic Wad al-Hayara / Wadi al-Hijara, linked to the older Arriaca, rather than treating every detail of the popular etymology as settled fact.

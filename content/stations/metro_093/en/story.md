Canal station takes its name from the Canal de Isabel II, the water-supply institution and infrastructure whose presence is especially visible around Bravo Murillo, Cea Bermúdez and the wider Chamberí area. When the Line 7 extension reached this point in 1998, the regional transport documentation described the new station as serving the sports facilities and exhibition spaces of the Canal de Isabel II, among other nearby public facilities.

The deeper origin is Madrid's nineteenth-century water problem. For centuries the capital depended on local springs, wells, water carriers and underground water galleries, systems that could not keep pace with a growing city. The solution promoted in the mid-nineteenth century was to bring water from the Lozoya River, north of Madrid, by a long hydraulic system of dams, conduits, tunnels, aqueducts and siphons.

The decisive legal step came on 18 June 1851. The royal decree published in the Gaceta de Madrid ordered the government to execute the works for supplying Madrid with healthy water through a canal derived from the Lozoya River and explicitly gave it the name Canal de Isabel II. The project was backed by Juan Bravo Murillo and sanctioned under Queen Isabel II; the waters reached Madrid on 24 June 1858, marked by the inauguration of the first reservoir and the monumental fountain in San Bernardo.

The station's immediate landscape is also tied to later phases of the Canal. The Third Reservoir, near the station area, was planned because Madrid's consumption kept growing and existing storage was insufficient. Its history is more complex than a simple 1907-1915 construction date: excavation and earlier works began in the 1890s, an ambitious reinforced-concrete roof by José Eugenio Ribera collapsed in 1905, and the final, more conservative solution was completed in the following phase.

The name is therefore well supported as a place/infrastructure name rather than a direct dedication to Queen Isabel II herself. In practice, however, the chain of naming is layered: the station is named for the Canal de Isabel II complex, and the Canal itself was named in 1851 in honor of the queen. No former station name was found; the current name appears to date from the station's opening on 16 October 1998.

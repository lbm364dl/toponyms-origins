Chamberí station is named for the place above it: Plaza de Chamberí and the surrounding district. Metro de Madrid's historical material places the original station under the square, on the first line between Cuatro Caminos and Sol, opened on 17 October 1919.

The older name behind the station is not purely local in origin. Chamberí is widely treated as the Spanish form of Chambéry, the historic capital of Savoy, but the Madrid evidence does not settle exactly how the name was transferred. What is solid is the Savoyard/French connection; what remains uncertain is the social route by which it became a Madrid toponym.

One tradition links the name to María Luisa Gabriela de Saboya, the first wife of Felipe V, whose memory or associations with Savoy supposedly attached Chambéry to this northern edge of Madrid. Another explanation, reported from Antonio Ponz's eighteenth-century travel writing, connects the name with troops returning from the last Italian war, when Spanish-French forces had been active in Savoy. A further story attributes it to a Napoleonic regiment or camp during the War of Independence, but that version is chronologically weak because Chamberí appears before 1808.

The key chronological anchor is the 1761 Plano de Chalmandrier, a map commissioned under Carlos III and preserved by the Instituto Geográfico Nacional. Later nineteenth-century urbanization turned the old outskirts, gardens, tejares, workshops and modest housing into a district that became both working-class and increasingly urban. The station inherited that established place name when the metro reached the square.

Chamberí is also famous for what happened after naming. It closed to passengers on 22 May 1966 because Metro could not viably lengthen its curved platforms from 60 to 90 metres for longer trains, especially with Iglesia and Bilbao so close. Restored and reopened as a museum in 2008, it is now the best-preserved window into the original 1919 Metro rather than an active stop.

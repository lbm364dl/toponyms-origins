Núñez de Balboa station is named for Calle de Núñez de Balboa, the street reached by one of the station entrances and listed by the official transport authority as an access at number 85. The street name, in turn, commemorates Vasco Núñez de Balboa, so the station is a street-derived personal name rather than a name chosen directly for the explorer alone.

Vasco Núñez de Balboa was an Extremaduran explorer and conquistador, born in Jerez de los Caballeros around 1475. Madrid City Council's own commemorative material identifies him as the mariner and explorer remembered for the European discovery of the Pacific from its eastern side, and notes that a plaque to him was placed at Calle Núñez de Balboa 2 in 2013, during the fifth centenary commemorations.

The key episode behind his fame took place in September 1513. Balboa crossed the Isthmus of Panama from the Caribbean side and reached the waters then known to Spaniards as the Mar del Sur, the South Sea. Later tradition remembers the solemn act of possession in the water, but the more precise historical point is that the 1513 crossing opened a new European geographic understanding of the ocean beyond the American mainland.

The Madrid street belongs to the nineteenth-century Salamanca district grid and runs between Calle de Alcalá and the northern end recorded in the official street register, with numbering extending through Salamanca and into Chamartín. The Metro station opened on Line 5 as part of the Callao-Ventas extension in 1970. In 1986, the Line 9 link between Avenida de América and Sainz de Baranda added new platforms and a long interchange, including moving walkways, at Núñez de Balboa.

The origin of the station name is therefore well supported: current CRTM data places the station access on Calle de Núñez de Balboa, the official Madrid street register confirms the street, and municipal commemorative material explicitly connects that street with Vasco Núñez de Balboa. The main nuance is biographical, not toponymic: Balboa's final trial and execution under Pedrarias Dávila are often described sympathetically, but public wording text should avoid stating as fact that the charges were simply false unless a specific historical source is being cited for that interpretation.

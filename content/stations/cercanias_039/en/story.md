Soto del Henares station takes its name from the Soto del Henares neighbourhood in the eastern part of Torrejón de Ardoz. Renfe describes the stop as a halt in the homonymous neighbourhood, serving Soto del Henares, Mancha Amarilla, La Zarzuela, the Hospital de Torrejón and the Casablanca industrial area. The name is therefore not primarily commemorative: it is a place-name carried into the rail network from the urban area it serves.

The phrase itself is descriptive. The Real Academia Española defines soto as a place on a riverbank or in a fertile riverside plain that is populated by trees and shrubs, and gives its origin as Latin saltus, 'wood' or 'forest'. In Soto del Henares, that word is attached to the Henares, the river that gives form to the corridor east of Madrid and to many local names in the area.

There is also an officially protected natural place called Soto del Henares. The Community of Madrid's Decree 169/2000, published in the regional gazette on 2 August 2000, established preventive protection for a 332-hectare natural area named Soto del Henares in the municipalities of Alcalá de Henares and Los Santos de la Humosa. The decree describes it as one of Madrid's best-preserved riparian woods associated with clay bluffs and meanders of the Henares, and as part of the ecological corridor of the Jarama-Henares basin.

The station's immediate urban context is more recent. Torrejón's Soto del Henares development was issuing its first building licences in July 2006, and the rail stop was inaugurated on 31 August 2015, with the first train stopping there the following morning at 5:16. The municipality presented it as a long-demanded connection for the eastern neighbourhoods and for the hospital, while Renfe lists it on Cercanías Madrid lines C-2, C-7 and C-8.

The area also has a deeper archaeological layer. Archaeological work linked to the Plan Parcial Soto del Henares documented an extensive 'field of pits' of more than 20 hectares near the confluence of the Torote and Henares, with more than 1,000 excavated structures and occupation phases from the Neolithic, Chalcolithic, Bronze Age and Early Iron Age. That archaeology enriches the story of the place, but it is not the direct origin of the station name; the strongest explanation remains the modern neighbourhood name, rooted in an older riverside landscape term.

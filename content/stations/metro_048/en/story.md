Tetuán station is named for the Madrid district around it. When Line 1 was extended north from Cuatro Caminos on 6 March 1929, the new station was placed on Bravo Murillo near Marqués de Viana and became the line's northern terminus. Metro de Madrid's historical material identifies the extension as the Cuatro Caminos-Tetuán section and links the station to the old village or barrio of Tetuán de las Victorias.

The barrio name reaches back to the War of Africa of 1859-1860. Spanish forces commanded by General Leopoldo O'Donnell fought at Tétouan, in northern Morocco, and took the city in February 1860. In Madrid, the place-name Tetuán de las Victorias was applied to land around the Dehesa de Amaniel in memory of that victory and of the Moroccan city from which the campaign returned.

The common story says the returning soldiers camped there and that the neighbourhood grew from the camp. Municipal itinerary material supports the military encampment but makes it more precise: the troops camped in the Dehesa de Amaniel for two days in May 1860 before the planned triumphal entry, and crowds, including Queen Isabella II, came to see them. The lasting settlement seems to have been driven not simply by soldiers staying behind, but by merchants, visitors, workers, and migrants who built provisional and then permanent premises along the road.

That road was the old carretera de Francia, the main northbound route out of Madrid, later identified with Bravo Murillo. Early streets in the area carried names connected with the African campaign, including O'Donnell, Prim, Topete, Ceuta, and Wad-Ras; some have since changed. This makes Tetuán a transport name with several layers: a Metro terminus, an extramural working-class suburb, a nineteenth-century military commemoration, and, behind all of that, a Moroccan city whose older form is recorded by UNESCO as Titawin.

The deeper etymology of the Moroccan Tétouan is separate from the Madrid station's direct naming. UNESCO records the medina as formerly known as Titawin, and reference sources commonly connect that Amazigh/Berber form with 'eyes' or 'springs'. For the Madrid station, however, the securely supported origin is local and historical: it is named after the Madrid district/barrio, which was named for Tétouan, Morocco, through Spain's 1860 victory commemoration.

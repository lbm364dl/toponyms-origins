Arturo Soria station is named from its setting on Calle de Arturo Soria, not from an older village or natural place-name. The street honours Arturo Soria y Mata, the Madrid-born urbanist, journalist, geometer and entrepreneur whose name is inseparable from the Ciudad Lineal district.

Soria's idea was a city stretched along a transport spine. Instead of expanding Madrid only in concentric rings, he proposed a long urban band with a tramway, services, gardens and housing arranged along a linear route. The surviving avenue still carries that memory: a modern traffic corridor, but one whose name points back to a deliberately planned urban experiment.

The chronology matters. Madrid's municipal district history says Soria published the Proyecto de Ciudad Lineal around Madrid in 1892, and that the Compañía Madrileña de Urbanización was formed in 1894 to build it. The city's heritage catalogue records the first stone of the Ciudad Lineal on 16 July 1894 and places the first urbanisation works in 1897. The full project was intended to approach 50 kilometres, but the built section remained a much shorter first neighbourhood.

The station itself belongs to the later transport history of the area. The Community of Madrid's account of Line 4 says the Alfonso XIII to Esperanza extension, originally planned only as far as Arturo Soria, opened on 5 January 1979 with three new stations. That makes the Metro name a twentieth-century transport label attached to a street and district whose identity had already been shaped by Soria's urban project.

The basic etymology is therefore secure: Arturo Soria station commemorates Arturo Soria y Mata through the avenue that bears his name. Some details often repeated about him need care. The journal La Ciudad Lineal ran from 1897 to 1932, but Soria himself died in 1920; and while Le Corbusier's engagement with Soria's linear city is well documented in scholarship, a direct Frank Lloyd Wright influence was not confirmed by a strong source.

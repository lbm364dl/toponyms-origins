Tribunal station is named for the Tribunal de Cuentas, the Court of Audit of Spain, whose main headquarters stands at Calle Fuencarral 81 beside the station area. In everyday Madrid speech, “Tribunal” became a shorthand for the institution and its building, so the Metro name points less to a court in the abstract than to a very specific landmark on Fuencarral.

The institution behind the name is Spain's supreme external auditor of public accounts and public-sector economic management. Its own history reaches back to medieval and early-modern accounting bodies, while the modern Tribunal de Cuentas was shaped in the 19th century and is now recognized in Spain's constitutional framework. The present building is also part of that story: it was designed by Francisco Jareño y Alarcón and built from 1860 to 1863 on a large trapezoidal block.

There is a second historic landmark in the name history: the Real Hospicio de San Fernando, across Fuencarral. Metro de Madrid archive material shows a 1918 construction plan for the “estación de Hospicio,” and later Metro heritage material explicitly explains that the project name came from the Royal Hospice located opposite the access. That building, largely rebuilt between 1721 and 1726 by Pedro de Ribera, is now the Museo de Historia de Madrid and is famous for its exuberant baroque portal.

The station belongs to Madrid Metro's original north-south line. The first line was officially inaugurated by Alfonso XIII on 17 October 1919 between Sol and Cuatro Caminos and opened to the public later that month; regional transport history lists this stop as Hospicio, now Tribunal. The interchange character came much later, when the former Suburbano was extended from Plaza de España to Alonso Martínez in December 1981, adding the platforms that are now part of Line 10 and linking with Line 1 at Tribunal.

The name origin itself is secure, but the chronology of “Hospicio” needs care. Some secondary sources say the station was originally called Hospicio, while Metro's own centenary catalogue phrases it more narrowly as the project name and identifies a 9 January 1918 construction plan under that title. Without inspecting early operating maps, tickets, signage, or company records, the safest public wording is that Hospicio was the documented project or very early name, and that Tribunal refers to the neighboring Court of Audit.

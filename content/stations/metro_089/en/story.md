Banco de España station is named for the Banco de España headquarters immediately beside it, one of the landmark buildings of Plaza de Cibeles. The Metro stop sits under Calle de Alcalá, between the Barquillo area and Cibeles, so the name works as a straightforward urban reference: passengers emerge beside Spain’s central bank.

Behind the station name is an institution older than the building itself. The Banco de España traces its institutional line to the Banco Nacional de San Carlos, founded in 1782 by royal warrant under Carlos III, and the current name Banco de España was adopted by law on 28 January 1856. In 1874 the bank was granted the monopoly on issuing banknotes in mainland Spain and its islands, and in 1921 banking legislation helped define it as a true central bank.

The Cibeles headquarters came later. The bank bought the former palace site of the Marquis of Alcañices at the corner of Calle de Alcalá and Paseo del Prado, and its architects Eduardo de Adaro and Severiano Sainz de la Lastra prepared the definitive project after a public competition failed to satisfy the works commission. The first stone was laid on 4 July 1884, and the building was inaugurated on 3 March 1891; official Banco de España material describes its style as eclectic-historicist rather than simply neoclassical.

The Metro history adds a useful twist. Metro de Madrid’s museum sheet places Banco de España on the inaugural Ventas-Sol section of Line 2, opened on 14 June 1924, and notes that the station had been part of plans connected with an unbuilt Gran Vía line. It also records that some original project names changed during construction: Castelar became Banco, while Independencia-Velázquez became Retiro. Castelar referred to the old name of today’s Plaza de Cibeles, but the public station name ultimately followed the bank that dominated the corner.

The name is therefore very secure, but some familiar anecdotes need care. The headquarters does contain the famous Cámara del Oro, and Banco de España publications identify it as part of the Cibeles headquarters; however, claims that it is exactly under the fountain or that it is among the world’s most secure vaults are usually journalistic or popular rather than necessary to explain the station name. The strongest evidence for the toponym is simpler and firmer: the station was named for the adjacent Banco de España building and institution.

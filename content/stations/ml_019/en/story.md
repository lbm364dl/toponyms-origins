The station name is direct and practical: **Campus de Somosaguas** identifies the Metro Ligero stop serving the Universidad Complutense de Madrid’s Somosaguas campus in Pozuelo de Alarcón. The Consorcio Regional de Transportes lists the stop on the Metro Ligero network, and Metro Ligero Oeste describes it as an ML2 station located on the UCM campus.

Behind the campus name is the older place-name **Somosaguas**, attached to the Húmera-Pozuelo landscape long before the tram arrived. Historical scholarship records forms such as **San Juan de Sumas Aguas** and explains the name in relation to abundant water. A study in *Anales del Instituto de Estudios Madrileños* cites early testimony saying that Sumas Aguas was so called for its many good waters.

The university layer is twentieth-century. UCM documentation states that the Campus de Somosaguas was created in 1968, and the university’s heritage page dates the main Economics building to that same year. The Faculty of Political Science and Economics had moved through San Bernardo and Ciudad Universitaria before the later separation of Economics and Political Science, with Economics already at Somosaguas by 1971.

The existing wording's political context is important, but it should be handled carefully. UCM’s own history page documents the anti-Franco student movement, the 1968 Raimon concert, and the later move of these faculties; press accounts explicitly describe the Somosaguas facilities as a way to distance one of the most combative university centres from Moncloa. That supports the interpretation, though it is better stated as a historically reported motive rather than as the sole proven reason for the campus name.

The medieval and environmental context makes the name richer. Pozuelo’s municipal history says the early settlement included the hamlets of San Juan de Somosaguas and San Pedro de Meaque, both depopulated during the conflict between Pedro I and Enrique II. Local historical writing also remembers a now-vanished Fuente de Somosaguas with ferruginous water, which fits the broader water-based reading of the toponym.

The station name comes first from place and use: Ramón y Cajal is the Cercanías stop serving the Hospital Universitario Ramón y Cajal in northern Madrid. Renfe explicitly describes the station as being next to the hospital of the same name, while Adif lists the station on the M-607 corridor, the railway edge of the hospital complex.

Behind that hospital name is Santiago Ramón y Cajal (1852-1934), physician, histologist and one of Spain’s most influential scientists. Official hospital anniversary material states that the hospital takes its name from Cajal, and that its inauguration on 18 October 1977 was made to coincide with the 43rd anniversary of his death.

Cajal’s fame rests on his microscopic study of nervous tissue. NobelPrize.org records that the 1906 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded jointly to Camillo Golgi and Santiago Ramón y Cajal for their work on the structure of the nervous system. CSIC’s biography emphasizes that Cajal’s descriptions and plates helped train generations of doctors and made him a founder of modern neuroanatomy.

There is also a Madrid science story behind the name. Cajal worked in the institutions that helped build Spain’s modern research infrastructure, including the Laboratorio de Investigaciones Biológicas and the Instituto Cajal. His surviving archive, drawings, preparations, photographs, instruments and personal objects are known as the Legado Cajal; the Museo Nacional de Ciencias Naturales describes a collection of 28,222 pieces, including 1,800 scientific drawings and more than 2,700 photographs.

The station itself appears in RENFE historical material as the Apeadero de Ramón y Cajal on the Madrid-Chamartín to Pitis line, opened under that name in 1984 to serve the Ciudad Sanitaria of the same name. The exact opening date needs a final check in the original RENFE memory because the searchable text gives both 29 May 1984 in a list and 3 June 1984 in the prose. The etymology, however, is secure: station to hospital, hospital to Santiago Ramón y Cajal.

Lago station takes its name from the Casa de Campo Lake, the broad sheet of water just outside the station area. This is not a commemorative name or a street-derived name: it points directly to the landscape feature that has long made this part of Casa de Campo easy to identify.

The lake itself began as an engineered water landscape rather than a natural lake. The City Council’s environmental education material explains that the present site had wet ground and seasonal water before the royal estate, but that in the 16th century five artificial ponds were built near the Meaques stream. These ponds were known in the 1656 Teixeira plan as Grande, Norte, Medio, Longuillo, and La Higuera.

That hydraulic system belonged to the royal Casa de Campo created around the court of Madrid. Felipe II moved the court to Madrid in 1561 and acquired the Vargas country house and surrounding estates soon afterward; later monarchs used the area for hunting, gardens, water management, fishing, boating, and winter skating. The park ceased to be Crown property in 1931, during the Second Republic, when it was transferred to Madrid City Council for public use.

The modern lake is the result of later change. By the 19th century, the larger surviving ponds had effectively joined into a single body of water, whose outline was reshaped into a more picturesque, irregular perimeter. Today it is a major recreational and sporting setting, with rowing boats, canoeing and kayaking facilities, restaurants, a fountain, and a prominent position in Madrid’s largest urban park.

The transport layer arrived in 1961, when the former Carabanchel Suburban Railway linked Plaza de España and Carabanchel. Archival and regional transport sources identify Lago as one of the surface stations on that line, later integrated into Metro line 10. The etymology is therefore secure, but the historical wording should emphasize the 16th-century pond system and later 19th-century reshaping rather than describing the lake only as an 18th-century creation.

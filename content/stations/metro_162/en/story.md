Manoteras station takes its name from the surrounding Manoteras area in the district of Hortaleza. The transport name is therefore local and geographic, not commemorative: Metro used an already established neighbourhood/place-name when line 4 was extended north-eastward to Pinar de Chamartín.

The older form most often cited is **Las Manoteras**. Local historical research and Madrid municipal material place an early reference in July 1807, when lands between Fuencarral, Hortaleza and Chamartín were auctioned and one place was named Las Manoteras. This matters because the name predates the modern housing estate and belongs first to a rural landscape of paths, small valleys, farms and high ground.

The usual etymological explanation is topographic. A note in Historias Matritenses, citing Jesús Rodríguez Morales and the philologist Emilio Nieto Ballester, derives Manoteras from Latin **lumba altaria**, passing through forms such as **Lomaotera** and **La Manotera**, and understands it as an allusion to high hills or elevated ground. A later book excerpt on Madrid station names gives the same plain meaning: the word can be translated as “high hills” and referred to a valley with market gardens surrounded by some of the highest rises in the area.

The modern neighbourhood developed much later. Madrid’s municipal Plan Integral de Barrio material describes Manoteras as originating in the 1807 Las Manoteras area and then changing through the Casa de Nazaret around 1900, the low houses of El Querol in the 1950s, and major protected-housing construction in the 1960s and 1970s. That history explains why Manoteras is both an old rural toponym and the everyday name of a contemporary urban neighbourhood.

The station opened on **11 April 2007** as part of the line 4 extension toward Pinar de Chamartín. The core claim that the station is named after the Manoteras area is secure. The deeper Latin derivation is probable and well aligned with the landscape evidence, but it should be presented as the accepted philological explanation unless the exact entry in Nieto Ballester’s dictionary is directly checked.

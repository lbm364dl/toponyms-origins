Velázquez station is best understood as a street-name station. Metro de Madrid's own historical sheet places it on Calle Goya between Calle Velázquez and Calle Castelló and explains that Calle de Velázquez is named in honor of Diego Rodríguez de Silva y Velázquez. The station therefore commemorates the painter through the surrounding urban toponymy, not through a separately documented Metro dedication.

The older name behind the station belongs to the Salamanca district's nineteenth-century expansion. Calle de Velázquez became one of the important north-south streets of that planned district, parallel to Serrano, and Metro notes that it originally had a central boulevard later removed as road traffic grew in the twentieth century. Naming such a prominent street after Velázquez fitted a Madrid habit of turning national history, literature, politics and art into everyday urban geography.

Diego Rodríguez de Silva y Velázquez was born in Seville in 1599 and died in Madrid in 1660. Prado and Royal Academy of History references identify him as the Sevillian painter who entered royal service after moving to Madrid, where from 1623 he worked for Philip IV's court. His career was not only artistic but also palatine: over time he rose within the royal household, and his late status helps explain why his image in Las Meninas carries social and political meaning as well as artistic self-consciousness.

The work most closely associated with his name is Las Meninas, painted in 1656 in the Alcázar of Madrid and now in the Museo Nacional del Prado. The Prado describes it as a complex court portrait in which Velázquez represents himself painting while the Infanta Margarita, her attendants and reflected images of Philip IV and Mariana of Austria occupy the same pictorial world. The later red cross of the Order of Santiago on Velázquez's chest is an important symbol, but the popular story that Philip IV personally painted it should be treated as tradition rather than established fact.

For the station name itself, the evidence is strong: Metro's historical material directly connects the station's location with Calle de Velázquez and states that the street honors the painter. The exact municipal date when the street name was assigned was not found in the accessible sources inspected here. The station's 1944 opening is clear, with an official inauguration on 23 March and public service beginning the following day according to Metro's historical account.

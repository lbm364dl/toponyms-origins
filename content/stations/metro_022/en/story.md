Diego de León station is named after the Madrid street at its original eastern end: Calle de Diego de León, at the junction with today's Francisco Silvela and Conde de Peñalver. When the Goya-Diego de León branch opened on 17 September 1932, Metro's own historical material placed the station at that meeting point and recorded an access by Calle de Diego de León. The transport name therefore preserves a street name first, and through it a nineteenth-century biography.

The person behind the street was Diego Antonio de León y Navarrete, born in Córdoba on 30 March 1807 and executed in Madrid on 15 October 1841. He belonged to a noble military family and became one of the best-known cavalry commanders of the First Carlist War. His reputation came from repeated charges with the Húsares de la Princesa and other cavalry forces in campaigns fought for Isabel II and the regency of María Cristina against the Carlist cause.

The title most attached to him, Count of Belascoáin, came from his actions around Belascoáin in Navarre. Later accounts turned him into a Romantic military figure: brave, elegant, loyal to his cause and doomed by politics. A Madrid municipal agreement of 21 July 1880 gave his name to the Salamanca-district street that runs from Serrano to Francisco Silvela, embedding that memory in the growing nineteenth-century city.

His end belongs to the crisis after the First Carlist War. In 1841, after María Cristina had gone into exile and Baldomero Espartero held the regency, León joined a moderate military conspiracy connected with O'Donnell and other officers. The plan included an attack on the Royal Palace and an attempt to take the young Queen Isabel II and her sister María Luisa Fernanda, but the action failed after resistance inside the palace.

León was arrested near Madrid, condemned by court martial and executed on 15 October 1841 near the Puerta de Toledo area. The familiar version says he faced the firing squad in dress uniform and declared that he did not die as a traitor; more detailed sources give related but not identical wording and gestures. The station name itself is not contested, but the public story should distinguish the well-supported facts from the later heroic legend.

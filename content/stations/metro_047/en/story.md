Cuatro Caminos is one of Madrid Metro's most literal names: it means “Four Roads.” The station is named after the Glorieta de Cuatro Caminos, the roundabout and crossroads directly associated with the station, rather than after a person, saint or political event.

The older place-name describes a junction on the northern edge of Madrid. Metro de Madrid's historical station sheet explains the crossroads through the large avenues and streets that meet there: Bravo Murillo, formerly the road toward Irún; Santa Engracia; Reina Victoria, formerly the Vereda de Aceiteros; and Raimundo Fernández Villaverde, formerly the Paseo de Ronda. A Madrid toponymic tradition preserved in Madripedia from María Isabel Gea's dictionary is more precise about the older naming logic: it says the name came from the Aceiteros road, the crossing of the road to France along Bravo Murillo, and Santa Engracia, while later streets altered the physical junction.

The Metro station made that crossroads a founding place of modern Madrid transport. Cuatro Caminos formed part of the first Metro de Madrid section, inaugurated on 17 October 1919 between Sol and Cuatro Caminos. At that moment it was the northern terminus of the first metro line in Spain and helped connect the central city with a rapidly urbanising, working-class area beyond the older core.

The name also gathers several layers of urban history. The area around the roundabout was once a peripheral landscape of roads, arrabales, services and later new housing and industry. Metro's historical material links the station's strategic location with the first depots and workshops of the network and with the urban development of Avenida de la Reina Victoria and the area around the old Stadium Metropolitano.

There is a useful nuance: the “four roads” are not simply a timeless list of the modern street mouths visible today. Sources differ slightly because they are describing different moments in the junction's evolution; the best-supported interpretation is that Cuatro Caminos is a descriptive historic toponym for the crossroads, later stabilised as the station name from the 1919 opening. The roundabout itself had other official names in the twentieth century, including Ruiz Giménez and Catorce de Abril, but the Metro station has not been found under a former public name.

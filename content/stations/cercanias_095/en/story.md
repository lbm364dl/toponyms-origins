Zarzalejo station is named for the municipality of Zarzalejo, in the Sierra Oeste of Madrid. Adif places the station at Calle del Ferrocarril in Zarzalejo, and Renfe's station sheet identifies it as station code 10204 in the Cercanias Madrid area. The railway name therefore does not commemorate a person or an event: it carries the older municipal place-name onto the rail network.

The place-name itself points to vegetation. A Madrid regional historical guide says the origin of Zarzalejo, or El Zarzalejo in some eighteenth-century documents, raises little doubt because researchers agree it is a phytonym from zarza. In everyday Spanish, zarza is a bramble or blackberry bush, while zarzal is a place covered with brambles. Zarzalejo can be read as a small or secondary bramble place, using the same kind of collective and diminutive formation seen in other Spanish plant-place names.

The name sits in a landscape where this kind of origin makes sense. Zarzalejo is a mountain municipality below Las Machotas, with traditional fields, pasture, stone, and scrubland in its historical setting. The regional guide quotes the nineteenth-century writer Andrés Marín, who connected the name with nearby bramble patches that produced blackberries. That explanation is not a founding document, but it shows that the zarza interpretation was already circulating in historical-local writing by the late nineteenth century.

The railway added a second layer to the name. The El Escorial-Ávila section opened on 1 July 1863, linking Zarzalejo by rail with Madrid, and the lower settlement around the railway became known as Zarzalejo Estación or Los Pajares. Today the station name on signs is simply Zarzalejo, but locally it also distinguishes the station-side settlement from Zarzalejo Pueblo, the older upper village.

There is also a more legendary explanation: local tradition says shepherds saw an apparition of the Virgin on a bramble, on the spot where the church would later stand, and that the bramble gave the municipality its name. This is culturally valuable, but it is not as strong as the linguistic evidence. The best-supported reading remains a place-name from zarza/zarzal, with El Zarzalejo as an older documented variant for the place rather than a separate station name.

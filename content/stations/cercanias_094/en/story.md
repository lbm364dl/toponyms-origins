The railway stop is best understood as a station named after a nearby place, not after a person or event. Current railway documentation identifies it as Las Zorreras-Navalquejigo, placing the stop in the Navalquejigo area of El Escorial and preserving the older local name Las Zorreras.

Behind that place-name is a transparent Spanish word. A zorrera is a fox den, from zorro or zorra, fox. For that reason, Las Zorreras probably meant a place of fox dens or fox-associated ground in the wooded and rural edge of the Sierra de Guadarrama.

The name is older than the modern commuter service. The Consorcio Regional de Transportes de Madrid historical railway map lists Las Zorreras as an apeadero on the Madrid-El Escorial railway opened in 1861, and local municipal documentation records a Camino de Las Zorreras or Los Tejones whose oldest located documentary reference is from 1764. That makes the toponym part of the pre-suburban landscape of El Escorial, before later housing estates and Cercanías branding.

Las Zorreras is also more than a railway label. Archaeological work describes an Iron Age site spread over the toponym known as Las Zorreras or Los Cierros de la Asunción, in the municipalities of Galapagar and El Escorial. The area was largely urbanized between the 1950s and 1970s, so the station name now preserves a rural name in a landscape that has changed considerably.

The main caution is evidential. The station's derivation from the local toponym is well supported, and the linguistic meaning of zorrera is straightforward. What remains unproven is the stronger statement that the name records a documented historical abundance of foxes; that is plausible, but it should be presented as an inference unless a cadastral, archival, or local toponymy source says so directly.

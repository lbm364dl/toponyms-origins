El Pozo station is named for the neighbourhood of El Pozo del Tío Raimundo, usually shortened in everyday speech to El Pozo. The Community of Madrid describes the station as serving both Palomeras Bajas and El Pozo del Tío Raimundo, with its main pedestrian access on the El Pozo side. The word pozo simply means “well”; in this case, the name preserves the memory of a local well associated with a man known as Tío Raimundo.

The oldest layer of the name is not a royal dedication or a planned street name, but a rural landmark. Local historical work reports a 19th-century cadastral reference to Pozo de Raymundo/Raimundo near the Camino de los Yeseros and the Camino del Hormiguero, while a later oral tradition says that one of the early settlers called Raimundo built a house with a well attached to it. Those two explanations point in the same direction, but they are not identical: one suggests an already existing cadastral place-name, while the other explains how local speech remembered it.

The modern neighbourhood took shape on the edge of old Vallecas. Academic studies describe El Pozo del Tío Raimundo as a marginal parceling that formed mainly between 1940 and 1956, after earlier land sales in the area from 1925. Migrant families from rural Spain bought or occupied small plots in a zone that was not legally prepared for housing and built shacks, then gradually improved them into more solid homes. By August 1956, published research gives about 1,714 shacks and roughly 7,600 residents, which made El Pozo one of the most striking examples of Madrid's post-war shanty urbanization.

The name also carries a powerful social history. In 1955 the Jesuit José María de Llanos Pastor moved into the neighbourhood; Madrid's municipal monument catalogue remembers him as the “Cura Rojo” because of his later militancy in Comisiones Obreras and the Communist Party of Spain, and as a leading figure in the regeneration of the area. The neighbourhood's urban renewal was not simply imposed from above: it became tied to resident organization, claims for services, and the long campaign to replace precarious housing with proper streets, schools, facilities, and homes.

The railway station is a late chapter in that story. It entered service in October 1996, between Entrevías and Vallecas on the Atocha-Guadalajara railway, and contemporary press reported that the new stop let residents reach Atocha in about ten minutes. No earlier official station name has been found; the current name appears to have been assigned when the station opened. The etymology is therefore secure at the station level, while the exact 19th-century origin of “Tío Raimundo” should still be treated as probable rather than fully proven until the original cadastral documentation is consulted directly.

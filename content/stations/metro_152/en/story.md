The Metro station **Juan de la Cierva** takes its public name from the Getafe neighbourhood where it stands. The Comunidad de Madrid describes the station as being on Avenida de España, by Calle Gerona, "in the neighbourhood that gives it its name." Behind that neighbourhood name is Juan de la Cierva y Codorníu, one of the best-known Spanish names in twentieth-century aviation.

Juan de la Cierva was born in Murcia in 1895 and trained as a civil engineer. His lasting contribution was the **autogiro**, an aircraft lifted by an unpowered rotating wing and pushed forward by a conventional propeller. It was not a helicopter in the modern sense, but its rotor technology became crucial to the later history of rotary-wing flight.

Getafe is not incidental to the name. The Museo virtual de Getafe states that an avenue and a neighbourhood bear Juan de la Cierva’s name because some of his test flights were made in Getafe. Centenary material on the autogiro identifies the successful C-4 flight of 17 January 1923 at the present Getafe Air Base as a key moment, with Captain Alejandro Gómez Spencer as pilot.

The station opened with MetroSur, the circular Line 12 serving the large municipalities south and southwest of Madrid. Its interior also reinforces the dedication: the Comunidad de Madrid records a station mural titled **"Homenaje a de la Cierva"**, by Carlos Alonso Pérez and Luis Sardá de Abreu, placed prominently in the vestibule above the Line 12 tracks.

The basic etymology is therefore well supported, but with one useful nuance: the station was not necessarily named by a direct biographical decree for the inventor; it was named for the neighbourhood, whose name commemorates him. Some sources differ on the exact date and place used to describe the first C-4 flight, with 17 January 1923 at Getafe strongly supported by autogiro-centenary material, while a BOE heritage notice mentions 31 January 1923 at Cuatro Vientos. That makes the person origin secure, but the detailed flight chronology should be phrased carefully.

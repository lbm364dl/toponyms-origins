The station name **Argüelles** comes from Agustín de Argüelles Álvarez, the liberal Asturian politician remembered as one of the great parliamentary voices of the Cortes of Cádiz. Metro de Madrid’s own historical file says that the name refers to Argüelles and places the old focus of that name around the statue that stood near the meeting of Alberto Aguilera, Marqués de Urquijo and Princesa.

Argüelles was born in Ribadesella in 1776 and became famous in Cádiz for his eloquence, which earned him the nickname “El Divino.” The Junta General del Principado de Asturias notes that he was elected as a deputy for Asturias in Cádiz in 1810 and ratified by the Cortes in 1811. He was entrusted with the preamble and a large part of the text of the 1812 Constitution, the landmark liberal constitution known popularly as La Pepa.

His political career was marked by both reform and repression. Madrid’s municipal heritage catalogue credits him with support for measures such as freedom of the press and the abolition of torture as a means of proof in criminal proceedings, and records his later participation in the constitutional work of 1837. After the absolutist restoration under Ferdinand VII, he suffered imprisonment and later exile in London before returning during the regency of María Cristina.

The urban name was already established before the metro arrived. The city’s monument to Agustín Argüelles was erected in 1902 for the coronation celebrations of Alfonso XIII, and the municipal catalogue says that from that moment it gave its name to one of Madrid’s central districts. When Metro opened the line 3 station in 1941, it was serving a district whose name already carried that liberal nineteenth-century memory.

There is no serious competing theory for the station name. The useful nuance is that the station is not simply named after a nearby street sign: it takes the name of a whole urban area associated with Argüelles’s statue and legacy. Metro’s chronology also shows that today’s interchange is the result of several stages: line 3 in 1941, line 4 in 1944, and line 6 in 1995.

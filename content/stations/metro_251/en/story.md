The Metro station name comes first from the city around it. Alsacia opened on Line 2 on 16 March 2011, when Metro was extended eastward from La Elipa to Las Rosas, and official project material lists Alsacia as one of the four new stations. Its location was not an invented Metro label: municipal documents already referred to the new station and intermodal area in Plaza de Alsacia.

Behind the Madrid square is Alsace, a historic region of the upper Rhine, today in eastern France along the German border. The Spanish form, Alsacia, is the normal Spanish name for the region. In the station area it fits a local street-name landscape that includes other European geographical names nearby, such as Aquitania, Suecia, Ginebra and Guadalajara.

Alsace’s history explains why the name carries more weight than a simple map label. The Peace of Westphalia in 1648 gave France important rights and influence in Alsace, but French control was not a single clean transfer of the whole region: Strasbourg, for example, was occupied by France later, in 1681. In the modern period, Alsace-Lorraine was ceded by France to Germany in 1871 after the Franco-Prussian War, returned to France after the First World War, absorbed by Nazi Germany during the Second World War, and restored to France in 1945.

That layered border history made Alsace one of the best-known symbols of Franco-German rivalry. After 1945, the same frontier also became part of a different story: France and Germany turned from repeated conflict toward institutional cooperation, with the 1963 Elysée Treaty later presented by French diplomacy as a symbol of reconciliation and a foundation for European integration.

For the station itself, the evidence is strongest at the local level: official Madrid and Community of Madrid sources verify the station’s 2011 opening, its Line 2 context and its relationship to Plaza de Alsacia. I found no official street-naming decree or municipal explanation assigning the plaza name specifically because of European reconciliation. The recommended reading is therefore: named directly after Plaza de Alsacia, ultimately after Alsace; the wider European-street and reconciliation reading is useful context, but should be presented as interpretation rather than as a documented naming motive.

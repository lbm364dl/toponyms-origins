Usera station takes its name from the district around it, not from a transport feature. The district, in turn, preserves the surname of Marcelo de Usera y Sánchez, the military officer and developer associated with the first urbanization of the older Usera neighbourhood south of the Manzanares. In that sense, the station name is a place name with a personal origin behind it.

The older story begins before the district existed. Much of this area belonged to Villaverde before its incorporation into Madrid, and official municipal material describes the land as former agricultural ground. The key family link was Carmen del Río Fernández, wife of Marcelo Usera and daughter of José del Río, a landowner remembered as the Tío Sordillo. Through that inheritance, the Usera name became attached to a landscape that was shifting from fields to housing.

The decisive change came in the 1920s. Municipal and district sources state that between 1925 and 1930 the land was subdivided and sold because building was considered more profitable than cultivation. The Real Academia de la Historia gives the wider biography: Marcelo de Usera y Sánchez was born in Madrid on 31 October 1874, served in the army until 1924, reached the rank of lieutenant colonel, and later worked in urban development as well as agriculture and livestock.

Usera's street map still makes that origin visible. Local histories and municipal summaries note that the layout and naming of many streets were connected with Usera's administrator and with the family circle: Isabelita Usera, Amparo Usera, Gabriel Usera, Mariano Usera, Nicolás Usera, Carmen del Río, and others. This is why the district can feel unusually genealogical: the surname is not only on the station but also embedded in the surrounding street network.

The metro station arrived later, when the name was already established locally. Line 6 reached Usera with the Pacífico-Oporto extension in May 1981, and the administrative district of Usera was created in the municipal reorganization of March 1987. Since the 2000s the area has also become strongly associated with Madrid's Chinese community and Lunar New Year celebrations, but that newer identity did not create the station name; it layered a contemporary cultural meaning onto an older toponym.

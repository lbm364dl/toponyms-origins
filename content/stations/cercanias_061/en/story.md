Aravaca station takes its name from the place it serves: Aravaca, now a neighbourhood of Madrid in the Moncloa-Aravaca district. In transport terms the name is straightforward: Adif, Renfe and the regional transport consortium identify the Cercanías stop as Aravaca, with service on the C-7 and C-10 corridors.

Behind the station name is an older settlement name. Aravaca was not always just a Madrid neighbourhood: it was a municipality of its own until the mid twentieth century, and Madrid City Council records its annexation to Madrid by the decree of 28 October 1949. Memoria de Madrid adds the practical chronology: the municipality kept its autonomy until its incorporation into the capital on 20 October 1951.

The medieval depth of the name is also important. Aravaca belonged to the historical jurisdiction of the Villa y Tierra de Madrid, and later references describe a Sexmo de Aravaca, one of the rural divisions of Madrid's territory. This means the station name preserves not only a suburban place-name but the name of an old territorial headland in the west and north-west of Madrid's historical district.

The difficult part is the origin of the word Aravaca itself. Joaquín Caridad Arias, in an article in *Anales del Instituto de Estudios Madrileños*, rejects the folk explanation based on 'arar' and 'vaca' and proposes that Aravaca is an ethnic designation connected with the Celtiberian Arevaci or Aravaci. He supports this with ancient references to the Arevaci, including Pliny's statement that the river Areva gave its name to the people, and with Roman epigraphic forms such as *Aravacorum*.

That proposal is plausible enough to record, but not secure enough to present as certain. Early-modern local tradition, recorded in the Relaciones Topográficas, offered the picturesque 'ara, vaca' story, which is valuable folklore but weak linguistics. A modern caution comes from Emilio Nieto Ballester, who notes that Aravaca could enter the discussion but should be studied in a wider series of similar forms, such as Aravacos, Alabacos and Labaco. For public wording, the safest wording is therefore: the station is named after Aravaca; the deeper place-name may preserve an ancient Celtic ethnic name, but that remains debated.

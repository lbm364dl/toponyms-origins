Gregorio Marañón station is named for Gregorio Marañón y Posadillo (Madrid, 19 May 1887 - Madrid, 27 March 1960). The immediate urban reference is the Plaza del Doctor Marañón area, at the meeting of José Abascal, Miguel Ángel, María de Molina and the Paseo de la Castellana; the station name therefore preserves a Madrid civic commemoration of the doctor rather than describing a hospital stop.

Marañón was a physician and scientist, especially associated with endocrinology, but he was also a historian, essayist, humanist and public intellectual. Authoritative biographical sources identify him as a doctor, scientist, historian, humanist and academician, and the Prado and scientific academy records emphasize his university chair, hospital work and membership in several Spanish academies.

His medical career was rooted in Madrid. He worked at the Hospital General or Hospital Provincial tradition, later remembered in the name of the Hospital General Universitario Gregorio Marañón, whose own official history calls him one of its most eminent professionals. He also moved well beyond medicine: his historical and biographical writing applied clinical habits of observation to figures such as Tiberius, Antonio Pérez, El Greco and the Count-Duke of Olivares.

The Metro station belongs to the expansion of the late 1990s. The Community of Madrid records the Line 8-Line 10 connection as put into service on 22 January 1998, with an intermediate station at Gregorio Marañón. A second official works page records the Avenida de América-Gregorio Marañón section of Line 7 as put into service on 13 February 1998, creating the Line 7 interchange with Line 10.

The etymology is therefore secure, but a few dataset details should be handled carefully. The station did not open in 1988; official transport chronology points to 1998. Claims about exact publication counts or the global rank of his Manual de diagnóstico etiológico should be presented cautiously unless a stronger bibliographic source is attached, although academic sources do confirm that the Manual was one of Marañón's most widely circulated medical works and was translated into many languages.

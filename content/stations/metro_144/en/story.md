The station name comes directly from Avenida de América, the broad road at the station's surface entrances and at one of Madrid's busiest north-eastern gateways. In this case the metro name is not a separate dedication: it is a transport name borrowed from the street and interchange around it.

The deeper name is América, the American continent. Spanish normally uses América as a continental name, and the Madrid street name fits a wider urban habit of giving major roads, squares and neighbourhoods names with international or Hispanic-American references. That makes the name easy to read as a continental or pan-American reference, although the exact municipal wording behind the avenue's original naming still needs archival confirmation.

The transport chronology is unusually important here. Metro de Madrid's 1973 annual-memory text, preserved by Memoria de Madrid, says that the Diego de León-Alfonso XIII extension of Line 4 was inaugurated on 26 March 1973 and included three new stations: Avenida de América, Prosperidad and Alfonso XIII. The same note already described Avenida de América as a future great metropolitan node where Lines 4, 6, 7 and eventually 9 would meet.

That prediction became the station's identity. Line 7 reached Avenida de América in 1975, Line 6 opened its platforms here in 1979, and Line 9 arrived in 1983 as part of the northern Line 9B section. The later bus interchange, inaugurated on 7 January 2000, made the name familiar far beyond the metro map: CRTM describes it as the hub for suburban and long-distance buses entering Madrid via the A-2 Barcelona road, together with Metro lines 4, 6, 7 and 9.

The current row is therefore right to connect the station to Avenida de América and to treat it as a place-name. The weaker part is the wording about the avenue 'honouring historical ties between Spain and Latin America': that is a reasonable interpretation, but I found stronger evidence for the geographic source of the name than for a precise commemorative act. It is also worth correcting the neighbourhood context: the station sits by the Salamanca-Chamartín edge and the avenue crosses several districts; the Hispanoamérica neighbourhood is relevant as nearby thematic toponymy, but not as the station's immediate surrounding neighbourhood.

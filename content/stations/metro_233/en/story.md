Arroyo Culebro station takes its name from the Arroyo Culebro, the stream whose name also identifies nearby urban areas and infrastructure in the south of the Madrid metropolitan area. The official Line 12 information places the station in Getafe on Paseo Juan José Rosón, while the regional transport consortium lists the access at Calle de las Ciencias, 14. The station opened with MetroSur, the circular Line 12 serving Alcorcón, Móstoles, Fuenlabrada, Getafe and Leganés, on 11 April 2003.

The name behind the station is therefore older than the Metro stop. Getafe’s municipal museum describes the Arroyo Culebro as one of the two main watercourses in the municipality, together with the Manzanares. It says the stream has its origin at the Mari Pascuala lagoon in Polvoranca, within Leganés, and that a long southern stretch passes through Getafe. Other official environmental documentation places the stream between Getafe and Pinto, gives it about 28 km, and states that it rises in Leganés and flows into the Manzanares.

Linguistically, the name is transparent but still needs care. The Real Academia Española defines culebro as a now obsolete masculine noun meaning culebra, “snake,” while culebra itself is traced to Latin colubra. That supports a literal reading such as “Snake Stream” or “stream of the snake.” What the sources do not prove is the common extra step: that the name was chosen specifically because the channel looks serpentine on the landscape.

The arroyo also helps explain the area’s recent urban geography. The 1989-1991 Programa de Actuación Urbanística “Arroyo del Culebro” appears in official state records for land in Getafe, Leganés and Pinto, and press coverage in the 1990s described “Operación Arroyo Culebro” as a major southern development corridor. By the time MetroSur arrived in 2003, Arroyo Culebro was not just a stream name but a recognized place-name used for neighborhoods, parks, industrial areas, sanitation works and transport stops.

The landscape has deeper history too. Getafe’s virtual museum records an archaeological site at Arroyo del Culebro discovered through the 1987 archaeological survey, with Iron Age material in an area affected by gravel extraction and later works near the stream. The safest public etymology is therefore simple: the station is named after the Arroyo Culebro hydronym and its associated urban area. The snake meaning is well supported by Spanish lexicography; the precise original motive for the hydronym remains unconfirmed.

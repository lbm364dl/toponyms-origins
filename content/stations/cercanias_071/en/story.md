El Escorial station takes its name from the municipality of El Escorial, northwest of Madrid, where the railway station stands. Modern transport sources identify the stop as El Escorial and place it in that municipality, with Cercanías service and connections to the wider rail network.

Behind the station name is an older place name, documented in forms such as Escurial and Escorial. The main current specialist explanation treats escorial as a collective formation in -al from escoria, ultimately Latin scoria: a place associated with slag, ash, or waste from metalworking or related activities. That reading fits the ordinary Spanish word escorial, which the RAE defines as a place or heap where metallurgical slag is deposited.

This explanation has deep historical roots. The Jeronymite chronicler Father José de Sigüenza already connected the name with scoria in the early seventeenth century, mentioning ashes and slag near the site of the royal monastery. Toponomasticon Hispaniae now supports the same broad family of explanations, pointing especially to the Bosque de la Herrería and its older name, the Dehesa de las Ferrerías de Fuentelámparas.

There is, however, an important rival theory. Martín Sarmiento argued in the eighteenth century that Escurial came from aesculus or esculus, a Latin tree name associated with oaks, holm oaks, or gall oaks; modern municipal material from El Escorial also presents the town’s origin as rooted in a wooded landscape of ésculos, carvajos, or quejigos. Jairo J. García Sánchez’s Cervantes-centre article likewise gives El Escorial as an example of a tree-based place name derived from aesculus.

For public wording, the safest wording is therefore layered: the station is certainly named after the municipality; the municipality’s name is probably best explained today by the scoria/escoria theory in Toponomasticon Hispaniae, but the oak/esculus theory remains a documented and locally favoured alternative. The railway name itself appears to have been in use from the nineteenth-century station, while the municipality’s official naming history includes forms such as El Escorial de Abajo before the twentieth-century standardization as El Escorial.

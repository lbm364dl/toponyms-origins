The Metro station called **Begoña** is named from its immediate urban setting: the Virgen de Begoña housing area beside the northern end of Paseo de la Castellana, near the La Paz hospital complex. Current transport information from CRTM lists Begoña on Metro line 10 and gives access points at Ciudad Sanitaria La Paz and San Modesto, matching the station's position between the hospital side and the Begoña neighbourhood side.

The deeper name is religious. **Nuestra Señora de Begoña**, or Our Lady of Begoña, is the Marian devotion associated with the sanctuary on the heights of Bilbao. The sanctuary itself presents the Virgin of Begoña as patron saint of Bilbao and Biscay, and the devotion is popularly known in Biscay as the **Amatxu**, 'the mother'. Madrid's station name therefore carries a Bilbao-Biscay devotional name into the capital through a local neighbourhood name.

The Madrid place-name belongs to the post-war history of public housing. Municipal and architectural sources identify the area as the **Poblado de Absorción** or **Unidad Vecinal de Absorción Virgen de Begoña**, designed from 1957 and built through the following years by public housing bodies. The city sources describe it as part of Madrid's rehousing and emergency housing operations, a dense peripheral development placed in a difficult position between major roads, railway infrastructure, hospitals and industrial areas.

That context matters because it corrects a simple devotional reading of the station. Begoña is not just the name of a saintly image placed on a Metro map; it is also the name of a specific mid-20th-century neighbourhood shaped by Madrid's rapid growth and by the housing policies used to absorb population pressure. Nearby La Paz opened in 1964 and later became one of the great hospital complexes of Madrid, so the station now serves both a residential memory of the 1950s-60s and a major health-care landscape.

One part of the existing explanation should be treated cautiously. The name **Begoña** is certainly toponymic before it is a personal or station name, but its linguistic etymology is disputed. A modern name guide notes that the popular segmentation **bego oina**, often glossed as a phrase about being 'at the feet', is considered baseless by Euskaltzaindia; it also reports Patxi Salaberri's view that many names ending in **-oña** may come from personal names. For the station, the strongest evidence is therefore the Madrid neighbourhood and the Marian dedication, not a precise Basque literal meaning.

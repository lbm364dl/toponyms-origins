El Bercial station takes its name directly from El Bercial, the Getafe neighbourhood where it stands. The transport evidence is straightforward: the regional transport authority lists the station as El Bercial, on Metro line 12, with access on Avenida Comandante José Manuel Ripollés in Getafe. The station opened as part of MetroSur on 11 April 2003, when Line 12 connected several southern Madrid municipalities in a circular metro route.

The older story is not the metro station but the place-name. In Spanish, a **bercial** is a place populated by **berceos**. The Real Academia Española defines berceo as equivalent to barceo, and barceo/albardín as an esparto-like grass of the Spanish steppes. That makes El Bercial a landscape name: a place where this coarse grass or scrub was noticeable enough to name the land.

Local Getafe archival material gives the strongest station-specific context. A municipal-archive text on El Bercial explains the name through berceo, barceo or albardín, and says the municipal documentation has clear, uninterrupted references to the place-name from the eighteenth century. It also notes related microtoponyms such as a small watchtower or **atalayuela** associated with El Bercial in the Catastro of the Marqués de la Ensenada.

The neighbourhood, however, is much more recent than the name. The same local account places the beginning of the modern urban development on 15 February 1956, when a planning sketch for the estate called El Bercial was approved; later planning and housing followed around the San Jorge estate and industrial-worker housing. Other local history notes connect the first residents with the late 1950s and with workers from factories such as Lanz Ibérica, in a district separated from central Getafe by railway and road barriers.

There is one caution. A public school page in the neighbourhood says the origin is not known with certainty and reports an older map form, **Hervestial**, which some people connect with the current name. That possibility should be recorded, but the botanical explanation is better supported by the dictionary meaning of bercial and by local archival interpretation. The current dataset should therefore correct the “watercress area” gloss: berros are watercress, while berceos/barceos are esparto-like grasses.

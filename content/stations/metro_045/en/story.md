Cruz del Rayo station is named after the surrounding historic place-name, above all the Colonia Cruz del Rayo in Chamartín. The Metro stop opened on Line 9 in the 1980s, but the name it carries belongs to an older urban landscape around El Viso and Prosperidad.

The best-known explanation is a local legend. Madrid’s municipal account says that a cross standing in the area, set on a stone pedestal, was destroyed by lightning and gave the place its name. Other popular versions describe the cross as iron and say it was deformed or damaged rather than destroyed. Because these versions are traditional rather than archival proof, the cross story is best treated as the origin legend of the toponym, not as a verified event.

What is much better documented is the later urban history of the name. COAM’s architectural guide records Colonia Cruz del Rayo as a project by Eduardo Ferrés y Puig, with project and works dated around 1927-1929, promoted by the Real Institución Cooperativa de Funcionarios del Estado, Provincia y Municipio. The colony was built as low-rise housing with gardens, curved and star-shaped street planning, and collective services, following the garden-city ideal associated with Ebenezer Howard.

That made the name more than a picturesque label. The colony became one of Madrid’s most distinctive interwar residential experiments: a small enclave of houses, gardens, Art Deco and modernista details, and planned neighborhood facilities. The central space, formerly Plaza de Aunós and now Plaza de José Castillejo, still helps explain why the area feels different from the denser streets around Príncipe de Vergara.

For the station’s etymology, the safest wording is therefore: named after the historic Cruz del Rayo place-name and colony, whose traditional origin is a lightning-struck cross. Official and professional sources strongly support the colony, dates, promoter, architect, and protection history. The physical cross episode remains a local legend with variant details.

The light-rail station is named for its immediate street setting. CRTM places the stop at Calle del Príncipe Carlos, and its station area map shows Calle de Vicente Blasco Ibáñez next to the access area, so the station name is best understood as a shortened reference to that street.

Behind the street name stands Vicente Blasco Ibáñez, born in Valencia in 1867 and dead in Menton, France, in 1928. He was not only a novelist: the Biblioteca Nacional de España describes him as a journalist and republican politician, a founder of El Pueblo and a national deputy in six legislatures between 1898 and 1907.

His literary career made the name travel far beyond Valencia. Works such as Arroz y tartana, Cañas y barro and Los cuatro jinetes del Apocalipsis built his reputation, and the last of these became especially visible abroad after the 1921 Hollywood film version directed by Rex Ingram. The American Film Institute notes that the film made Rudolph Valentino an instant matinee idol.

The Madrid transport name belongs to a much later urban layer. ML1 was opened on 24 May 2007 to connect Pinar de Chamartín, Sanchinarro and Las Tablas, and the Community of Madrid describes Blasco Ibáñez as one of the stations on the Sanchinarro-Las Tablas section. The setting is Sanchinarro, within the Valdefuentes neighbourhood of Hortaleza, not Fuencarral-El Pardo.

The etymology is therefore secure at the person level, but the exact administrative act that assigned the street name has not been inspected. A Hortaleza district record shows the Calle Vicente Blasco Ibáñez area in connection with a project definitively approved on 23 December 1998, confirming that the odonym was already in use before the light-rail station opened.

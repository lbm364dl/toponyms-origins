Rubén Darío station is best understood as a Metro name borrowed from the city around it. Its entrances open onto the Glorieta de Rubén Darío and the nearby Paseo de la Castellana, and the official CRTM station page lists accesses at the Glorieta itself. The public transport name therefore preserves a pre-existing Madrid street name rather than creating a new dedication in 1970.

The person behind the name was Félix Rubén García Sarmiento, known to literature as Rubén Darío. Born in Metapa, Nicaragua, in 1867 and deceased in León, Nicaragua, in 1916, he was a poet, journalist and diplomat. The Instituto Cervantes describes him as the foremost representative of Spanish-language Modernismo, a movement that renewed rhythm, imagery and cosmopolitan ambition in poetry written in Spanish.

Madrid mattered in Darío's life. In 1892 he travelled to Europe and, in Madrid, joined the Nicaraguan diplomatic delegation for the commemorations of the discovery of America. Later, his work and journalism brought him back into Spanish literary circles: the Cervantes biography links him with young admirers such as Juan Ramón Jiménez, Valle-Inclán and, in Paris, Antonio Machado, and notes that *Cantos de vida y esperanza* was published in Spain in 1905. From 1907 to 1909 he also served as Nicaragua's resident minister in Madrid.

The urban name predates the Metro station by almost half a century. Madrid's official callejero records the Glorieta de Rubén Darío as a current street name with an entry date of 7 October 1921, while a 1921 press item says the City Council had approved replacing the name Glorieta del Cisne with Rubén Darío. The later station, opened with Line 5's Callao-Ventas section in 1970, took over that established place name.

The evidence is strong for the dedication, but the wording needs care. It is fair to call Darío the central or leading figure of Modernismo; saying that he founded it single-handedly overstates a broader literary movement. It is also more precise to say that the Metro station is named through the Madrid square, which itself honors Darío, rather than implying that Metro independently chose the poet's name.

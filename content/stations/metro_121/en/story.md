Plaza de España station is named for the large square above and around it, not for a person or for the country in the abstract. The Metro name follows Madrid's Plaza de España, the civic space at the western end of Gran Vía and beside Calle de la Princesa, Calle de los Reyes and the historic axis toward the Royal Palace.

Before the modern square existed, this area was associated with San Marcial and the San Gil barracks. Madrid municipal heritage material describes the San Marcial parade ground in front of the barracks as the origin of the later Plaza de España. The San Gil barracks, linked to Francesco Sabatini's eighteenth-century royal Madrid, occupied a strategic high point near the palace and eventually became an obstacle to the westward growth and modernization of the city.

The name Plaza de España appears in the urban project that followed the barracks' removal. A digitized 1910 item from La Correspondencia de España, preserved by Memoria de Madrid, reports that the City Council considered forming a large square called Plaza de España on the plots left by the demolition of the old San Gil barracks. A later municipal historical study explains that after the 1908 demolition, the first urbanization plan was drawn up in 1911 and that, even though the plan itself was not fully realized, the name remained.

The square's best-known symbol is the Monument to Cervantes. The regional declaration of the monument as Bien de Interés Patrimonial explains that the idea had been discussed since 1905, that in 1915 the new Plaza de España was chosen as the site, and that the winning team was selected in 1916. The monument was only partially inaugurated in 1929 and was not completed until 1969, so the plaza's image was assembled over decades rather than in a single act.

Metro history adds another layer. The Line 3 station at Plaza de España opened with the Sol-Argüelles extension in July 1941; the deep Suburbano station, later incorporated into Line 10, opened on 6 February 1961 between Plaza de España and Carabanchel. The place-name is therefore very well supported, but two details in the existing wording should be tightened: the station should not be treated as a 1925 opening for lines 3 and 10, and the 1960s completion of the Cervantes monument should not be simplified as the late arrival of the bronze Don Quixote and Sancho figures.

Tirso de Molina station is named for the square above it, and the square is named for Tirso de Molina, the literary pseudonym of Fray Gabriel Téllez. Téllez was a Mercedarian friar, preacher, chronicler, poet and dramatist of the Spanish Golden Age. The name therefore commemorates a person, but through a very local Madrid landmark: the station sits under the public space that took his name after the Civil War.

Before that change, both the station and the square were associated with another name: Progreso. Metro de Madrid's historical material states that the station opened on 26 December 1921 as Progreso, following the name of the plaza at the time. The plaza itself had been created in the nineteenth century on the former site of the Convento de Nuestra Señora de la Merced, the Mercedarian convent whose memory is central to the later Tirso name.

Gabriel Téllez's biography is well documented in broad outline, though some details of his early life remain uncertain. PARES identifies him as Tirso de Molina, born in Madrid in 1579 and deceased in Almazán in 1648, and records him as a Spanish writer, dramatist and Mercedarian religious. He entered the Order of Mercy in 1600 and took the habit at the Monastery of San Antolín in Guadalajara; other sources also connect him with Madrid's Mercedarian convent, so the plaza's naming links the writer, the order and the lost convent site.

Tirso's public fame rests especially on his theatre. The Biblioteca Virtual Miguel de Cervantes presents him as one of the great comic and serious dramatists of seventeenth-century Spain and highlights *El burlador de Sevilla* as the origin of the Don Juan myth. A careful public account should say that the work is traditionally attributed to Tirso, because modern scholarship has debated the authorship of *El burlador de Sevilla* and related texts such as *Tan largo me lo fiáis*.

The current station name is not original. A metro chronology records the change from Progreso to Tirso de Molina on 10 July 1939, shortly after the end of the Civil War. That date makes the station part of a wider postwar reshaping of names and symbols in central Madrid: the liberal name Progreso disappeared, while the Mercedarian dramatist whose order had occupied the site became the public reference.

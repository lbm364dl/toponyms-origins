La Moraleja station is named after the La Moraleja area of Alcobendas, north of Madrid. The station is on Line 10 and today appears in the CRTM station listing as a B1-zone Metro stop with access at Avenida de la Ermita in Alcobendas. Its name points less to the platform site itself than to the nearby residential, business and urbanization landscape known by that older place-name.

The deeper name is not modern branding. Moraleja is best understood as a phytotoponym: a place-name formed from vegetation. Toponomasticon Hispaniae derives Moraleja from Latin MORA, with collective and diminutive suffixes, and explains mora as connected with the fruit of the moral, the morera and the zarzamora. In plain terms, La Moraleja probably meant something like a smaller or marked place associated with mulberry or related berry-bearing plants.

The name was already attached to land on the northern edge of Madrid long before the twentieth-century luxury development. A catalogue entry in the Comunidad de Madrid publication on Carlos III and the royal sites identifies an 1778 plan of the bosque llamado de La Moraleja, located between the villas of Alcobendas and Hortaleza. A local historical account of Hortaleza also notes that in 1778 Carlos III arranged the purchase of the Dehesa de La Moraleja in the term of Hortaleza.

The modern image of La Moraleja belongs mainly to the period after 1946, when the estate was planned as a garden-city style residential development. Alcobendas planning material describes La Moraleja as a clear example of a ciudad jardín model whose ordering began in 1946. That helps explain the station name: when MetroNorte reached Alcobendas in 2007, the inherited rural name had already become the public name of a high-status urban and business area.

The confidence is high for the station being named after the nearby La Moraleja area, and good but slightly more cautious for the ultimate linguistic origin. Toponomasticon Hispaniae records a competing proposal connecting Moraleja with old walls or constructions from vulgar Latin *muratella, but it judges the phonetic problems of that derivation severe. For public use, the vegetation explanation is the best-supported reading, with the royal-dehesa history presented as historical context rather than the literal origin of the word.

Quintana station is named for the place immediately above it: Plaza de Quintana, an official Madrid municipal location at Calle de Alcalá 339 in the Quintana neighbourhood of Ciudad Lineal. Metro de Madrid and municipal heritage material place the station in that square, so the direct transport-name origin is clear even if the deeper origin of the word needs more care.

The best-supported older layer is local rather than biographical. Municipal land-inventory documents repeatedly describe plots in Ciudad Lineal as lying between the estate called "Quinta de la Quintana" and Arroyo Calero. A parish history of old Canillas also lists "Barrio de la Quintana" among the areas covered before Canillas was annexed to Madrid in 1949. That makes a local estate or neighbourhood toponym the most likely root behind the modern square and station name.

The chronology also fits the urban history of eastern Madrid. Before this area became fully absorbed into Madrid's postwar city, it belonged to the fringe of Canillas and lay around the old road eastward, today's Calle de Alcalá. In the 1950s and 1960s, the surrounding residential neighbourhoods consolidated, and the metro arrived with the Ventas-Ciudad Lineal extension in 1964. The station later became part of Line 5 on 20 July 1970.

There is a tempting alternative explanation: Manuel José Quintana y Lorenzo, the Madrid-born poet, liberal politician, educator, senator and royal tutor, was a major nineteenth-century figure and is commemorated elsewhere in Spanish street names. The Real Academia de la Historia identifies him as minister, poet, philologist, journalist and senator, and his 1855 public coronation as a national poet is a real event in his biography. However, that does not by itself prove that this specific Ciudad Lineal plaza was named for him.

For public display, the safest wording is therefore: Quintana is named after Plaza de Quintana, whose name probably continues an older local place-name, Barrio or Quinta de la Quintana. The Manuel José Quintana explanation should be marked as unverified unless a municipal nomenclature file, street-naming agreement or historical callejero entry is found. The ordinary Spanish word "quintana" also has a genuine Latin origin, but that linguistic background is context for the place-name, not direct proof of why the metro station was named.

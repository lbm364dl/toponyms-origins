Arganda del Rey station is named for the municipality it serves, not for a separate street, person or monument. It is the eastern end of Line 9 and stands in the town of Arganda del Rey, one of the southeastern municipalities connected to Madrid by the 1999 Metro extension beyond Puerta de Arganda.

The older name behind the station is **Arganda**. In a survey of Madrid place-names, Jairo J. Garcia Sanchez describes it as one of the Community of Madrid's older, pre-Roman names and links it to the Indo-European base *arg-, meaning 'clear', 'white' or 'bright'. He notes that this root is also common in hydronyms, where it can refer to the silvery or crystalline appearance of water.

The second part, **del Rey**, belongs to the town's early modern history. Arganda had belonged to the Archbishopric of Toledo, but municipal history records that Philip II granted it royal-town status in 1583 after payment to the Crown. A municipal planning catalogue gives the more detailed sequence: in 1580 Philip II incorporated Arganda into the Crown, and in 1583, once payments were made, granted the Carta de Venta y Exencion Perpetua, the coat of arms and the royal surname.

The Metro station also sits on a transport story older than Metro itself. The Line 9 extension from Puerta de Arganda to Arganda del Rey opened on 7 April 1999, with four new stations and a distinctive concession model operated by Transportes Ferroviarios de Madrid. The corridor evokes the older Tajuña railway and the popular memory of the Tren de Arganda, remembered in the saying 'el tren de Arganda, que pita mas que anda'.

The secure part of the name is therefore straightforward: the station is named after the municipality. The deeper linguistic origin of Arganda is probable rather than absolutely proven, because very old pre-Roman names are difficult to pin down and other theories have circulated, including Arabic, Latin and mythological explanations. The current strongest public explanation is the Celtic/Indo-European *arg- account, with the warning that the royal qualifier should be tied to the sixteenth century, not simply to 1996.

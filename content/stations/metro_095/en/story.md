The Metro station is named directly after the Puerta de Toledo, the monumental gate standing at the Glorieta de la Puerta de Toledo. The transport link is straightforward: CRTM places the station access at that same square, and Line 5 has served this stop since the original Callao-Carabanchel section opened in June 1968.

The name reaches back beyond the Metro to Madrid's old southern approach. The present Puerta de Toledo replaced earlier gates with the same name that had stood nearby since at least the sixteenth century, marking the road toward Toledo and the south. In its current form it is not a defensive gate so much as a neoclassical triumphal arch, built in granite and Colmenar stone.

Its political history is more complicated than a simple royal monument. During Joseph Bonaparte's rule, an earlier triumphal arch was commissioned from Silvestre Perez in 1811-1812, but it was not completed. After the French left Madrid, the city council commissioned Antonio Lopez Aguado in 1813 for the present gate, initially connected with National Sovereignty and the constitutional moment of Cadiz. When Ferdinand VII returned and restored absolutism, the symbolic program was redirected toward the king and the defeat of Napoleonic France.

That tension is still built into the monument's story. The upper inscriptions present the 1827 gate as an offering to Ferdinand VII, while municipal heritage sources describe episodes in which copies of the Bayonne Constitution and the Constitution of Cadiz, along with coins, were placed in and removed from a commemorative box in the structure. The sculptural decoration was designed by Jose Gines and ultimately carved by Ramon Barba and Valeriano Salvatierra, with imagery of Spanish monarchy, military victory and the Villa of Madrid.

The station name is therefore well supported as a historical-place name: it points to the surviving Puerta de Toledo, not to a person called Toledo. The only important correction is nuance. It is better to say that Joseph Bonaparte's unbuilt project was the immediate predecessor, while the current gate was the 1813-1827 work of Antonio Lopez Aguado, officially completed in 1827 and now protected as a Bien de Interes Cultural.

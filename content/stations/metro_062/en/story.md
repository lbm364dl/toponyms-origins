Nuevos Ministerios station is named directly for the government complex above and beside it: the Nuevos Ministerios, or "New Ministries", on Paseo de la Castellana. It is therefore a place-name station, not a dedication to a single minister or architect, although both Indalecio Prieto and Secundino Zuazo are central to the story.

The name comes from a major Second Republic urban project. Madrid City Council's heritage catalogue states that Prieto, then Minister of Public Works, commissioned Zuazo in 1932 to design a group of monumental public buildings on the grounds of the old hippodrome. The plan formed part of the northward extension of Castellana and was meant to gather scattered state offices into a new administrative centre.

Work began in 1933, but the Civil War interrupted the project. After the war, Zuazo was purged by the new regime and the final work was assigned to other professionals, who broadly followed the original design while adding changes aligned with the new political order. Municipal and tourism sources usually give 1942 as the completion period for the complex, while later administrative occupation and adaptation continued after that date.

The transport history adds another layer to the name. The railway stop at Nuevos Ministerios opened first, in 1967, as part of the Atocha-Chamartín tunnel popularly known as the "túnel de la risa". Metro reached the site later: the first Metro station here opened with Line 6 on 11 October 1979, and later works incorporated Lines 8 and 10, turning the name into one of Madrid's best-known interchange labels.

The etymology is therefore secure: the station was named for the existing ministerial complex and the urban area identified by it. The current row is substantially correct, but public text should avoid implying that Franco simply created the complex from scratch; the stronger evidence shows a Republican commission and design, a wartime interruption, and postwar completion with modifications.

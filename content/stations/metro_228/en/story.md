The station name is local and direct. Loranca is the MetroSur stop serving the Loranca urbanization in Fuenlabrada, and the Community of Madrid describes the station as being inside that urbanization, on Calle de la Alegria. When Line 12 opened to the public on 11 April 2003, the stop kept the name already used by the surrounding residential district.

That modern district did not invent the word Loranca from scratch. It reused an old Fuenlabrada place name: the medieval hamlet or despoblado of Loranca. In the sixteenth-century Relaciones Topograficas, Fuenlabrada's witnesses explained that the town had been founded by people from two nearby depopulated settlements, Loranca and Fregacedos. Because that testimony was collected in the 1570s and says the move had happened about two hundred years earlier, historians place the formation of Fuenlabrada around the middle of the fourteenth century.

The name also points to a landscape older than the medieval village. Archaeological work in the Loranca area has documented late-Roman and Visigothic rural occupation, including two early medieval necropolises near the Camino del Bañuelo and close to the Aldehuela and Loranca streams. This does not prove that the Metro station name is Visigothic, but it shows that Loranca was not merely a late twentieth-century urban label: it belonged to a long-used rural territory.

The twentieth-century return of the name came through planning. Loranca Ciudad-Jardin was one of the major residential operations on Fuenlabrada's edge in the 1990s, planned as a large garden-city-style development near Móstoles and later served by MetroSur. Contemporary press and architectural sources describe the original project at roughly 7,000 to 7,300 homes, so the commonly repeated 12,000-home figure should be treated as a later or broader neighbourhood figure unless a specific planning document is cited.

The deepest linguistic etymology is less certain than the station's naming. A specialist article by Fernando Jimenez de Gregorio proposes that Loranca may come from the Celtic cognomen Laurus plus the suffix -anca, and notes medieval forms such as Loranque. No stronger inspected support for the current row's Arabic luran or loranco-tree explanations, so the safest public reading is: the station is certainly named for the neighbourhood, the neighbourhood preserves the medieval Loranca place name, and the older word origin remains probable rather than fully proven.

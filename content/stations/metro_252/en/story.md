La Almudena station is not named for a street in the old centre of Madrid. Its name points to the great cemetery beside it: the Cementerio de Nuestra Señora de la Almudena, on Avenida de Daroca. Madrid's regional page for the Line 2 extension describes the station as being next to that cemetery, and the city lists La Almudena metro as one of the ways to reach it.

The cemetery's name leads back to the Virgen de la Almudena, Madrid's patroness. The city tourism page states that the cemetery receives its name from the Virgin, and the full official cemetery name keeps the dedication clear: Nuestra Señora de la Almudena. In other words, the metro name is local and topographic at first, but its deeper origin is religious.

Behind the devotion is one of Madrid's central medieval legends. The word Almudena is usually explained from Arabic al-mudayna, meaning the citadel or fortified enclosure, a term associated with the early walled settlement around today's Royal Palace and cathedral. Tradition says that Christians hid an image of the Virgin in the defensive wall during the Muslim period and that it was revealed near the Puerta de la Vega after Alfonso VI took Madrid.

The chronology is less firm than the story. Some Madrid educational and cathedral materials give 1083 for Alfonso VI's entry into Madrid, while other tellings attach the rediscovery to 9 November 1085. The famous details of stones falling away, flowers or candles, and the image surviving in the wall belong to devotional tradition, not to a securely documented medieval record.

The station itself belongs to a much later chapter. It opened on 16 March 2011 as one of four stations in the Line 2 extension to Las Rosas, together with Alsacia, Avenida de Guadalajara and Las Rosas. Its name therefore links a modern transport stop to Madrid's eastern necropolis, and through that cemetery to the patronal cult, the Arabic place-name layer of medieval Madrid, and the city's annual Almudena celebration on 9 November.

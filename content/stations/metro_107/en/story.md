Plaza de Castilla station takes its name from the square directly above it, one of Madrid's major northern transport spaces. The name is therefore a place-name first: Metro adopted the name of the urban square when Line 1 was extended to this point in 1961.

Before it became a monumental roundabout, this was a road junction on the edge of the growing city. CRTM's historical account describes the present Plaza de Castilla as the old fork between the road from Madrid to France, now Bravo Murillo, and the road to Chamartín, with a toll point near kilometre 6 and roadside inns serving travellers and muleteers.

The modern square belongs to the postwar expansion of Madrid. In the mid-1940s a broad avenue was laid out north from Nuevos Ministerios, then named Avenida del Generalísimo; at its meeting with the old road to France, planners envisaged a large circular square known as Plaza de Castilla. Chamartín and other northern municipalities were annexed into Madrid around the same period, so the place moved from edge condition to capital-city landmark.

Metro made the name part of everyday Madrid geography on 4 February 1961, when Line 1 reached Plaza de Castilla from Tetuán. The interchange later gained the former Line 8, today's Line 10, in 1982, and the northern section of Line 9 in 1983. Above ground and below ground, the square became a gateway for buses from the A-1 Burgos road and the M-607 Colmenar road, while the Puerta de Europa towers, the Canal de Isabel II water deposit, courts and bus interchange gave it its modern silhouette.

The safest etymology is therefore direct and urban: Plaza de Castilla station is named after Plaza de Castilla. 'Castilla' points to Castile, the historic Spanish region and former kingdom, but I found no inspected official naming record that proves the plaza was specifically dedicated to the Crown of Castile. A public label should avoid overclaiming that narrower interpretation unless a municipal naming file is later checked.

The station name points to Manuela Malasaña Oñoro, one of the best-known popular figures of Madrid’s Dos de Mayo uprising in 1808. Line 12’s Manuela Malasaña station is in Móstoles, but its name reaches back to the centre of Madrid and to the violent repression that followed the revolt against Napoleonic troops.

Manuela is remembered as a young working woman from the old Maravillas area, later popularly known as Malasaña. Madrid municipal material describes her as an embroiderer who died at 17, and later memory made her one of the emblematic female victims of the War of Independence. Her surname also became part of Madrid’s urban language through the street and, by extension, the neighbourhood name.

The famous story has several versions. A romantic nineteenth-century version placed Manuela near the fighting at the Monteleón artillery park, helping her father with cartridges. Stronger documentary discussion, however, undermines that version: the Biblioteca Nacional de España notes that her father was already dead, and preserves the pension-claim account in which Manuela was returning from embroidery work when soldiers found the scissors she carried for her trade.

That detail matters because Marshal Murat’s repression treated ordinary objects as weapons. In the better-supported account, the scissors were enough for French soldiers to execute her summarily. The public memory of Manuela Malasaña therefore joins two ideas: patriotic resistance and the vulnerability of ordinary civilians caught in military terror.

The station’s immediate setting adds a modern Móstoles layer. The Consorcio Regional de Transportes lists Manuela Malasaña as a MetroSur Line 12 station in fare zone B2, and Móstoles officially lists the IES Manuela Malasaña at Calle Desarrollo 50 nearby. The school may help explain the local choice of name, but the documented origin to display should be Manuela Malasaña Oñoro herself; the school-link should be treated as context unless a formal naming file is found.

Barajas station takes its public name from the place around it: Barajas, now one of Madrid's districts and historically a village on the north-eastern approach to the city. The Community of Madrid describes the station as located by Avenida de Logroño and Plaza de Pajarones, in the Barajas area, and the Line 8 extension was built to connect the airport and the old village with the Metro network.

Before it was a Madrid district, Barajas was a separate municipality. The official decree dated 18 November 1949 approved the total annexation of the municipal term of Barajas to Madrid, and later municipal organization placed the area within Hortaleza until the 1987 restructuring created the present district of Barajas. That administrative history is the secure explanation for the station name: the Metro stop preserves the name of the former town.

The older meaning of the word Barajas is much less secure. Juan Ortega Rubio's printed compilation of the Relaciones Topográficas records the 1579 local account that the place was once called Baraja and that, in Muslim times, it was ruled by a Moor named Baraxa, son of Axa. This is valuable as evidence of what local informants believed in the late sixteenth century, but it is not documentary proof that such a person existed or that the name really came from Arabic or Hebrew elements.

Modern toponymic research is more cautious. Joaquín Caridad Arias argues that the Bar Axa explanation lacks historical documentation as an anthroponym or person and is better understood as a folk etymology. He also reviews the Latin *varalia* or 'framework of poles/hedge' interpretation associated with Menéndez Pidal and repeated by later authors, but questions whether a Romance explanation can account for the wider family of similar names across and beyond the Iberian Peninsula.

For public display, the safest reading is therefore layered: the station is definitely named for Barajas as a place; Barajas is securely the old municipality annexed to Madrid; and the remote origin of the place-name remains contested. The best-supported scholarly direction is pre-Roman, probably connected with ancient personal, divine, tribal, or place-name material, while the Bar Axa story should be presented as a memorable early tradition rather than as fact.

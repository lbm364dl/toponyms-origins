Alcorcón Central is named first of all for where it is: the central Alcorcón transport interchange on Metro de Madrid Line 12. The Community of Madrid describes the station as being in the centre of the municipality, between Avenida de Móstoles and Calle Berlín, beside the Cercanías Renfe station. The adjective Central distinguishes the Metro station from the wider municipal name and marks its role as a town-centre interchange.

Behind the station name lies the older place-name Alcorcón. The town council's history page records the first medieval reference on 28 July 1208, when the Cañada de Alcorcón appears as a sheep route and boundary marker for the Council of Madrid. That means the name was already attached to the landscape before the modern town and long before MetroSur.

The origin of Alcorcón is not settled. Local historical sources often explain it from Arabic forms such as al-gor, alcor or al-kur, meaning a hill, mound or pass; this fits the old settlement's high position and the idea of a lookout or defensive point near medieval Madrid. The municipality presents this theory while also stressing that early documentation is sparse and that much of the origin story is reconstruction.

A more specifically philological explanation connects Alcorcón with woodland rather than pottery or a watchtower. Álvaro Galmés de Fuentes, discussing Mozarabic place-names, treats Alcorcón as a hybrid form: Arabic al- attached to corco, a Romance word related to Latin quercus and used for oak, holm oak or cork oak. In that reading, Alcorcón would preserve a memory of an oak or cork-oak landscape, comparable to other Iberian place-names built on the same root.

Alcorcón's pottery tradition remains part of the story, just not the safest etymology. The town council notes that pottery was already a point of local pride in the sixteenth century and that the craft shaped Alcorcón's identity for centuries. For the station display, the best wording is therefore cautious: the Metro name is certainly taken from central Alcorcón; the municipal name is medieval, and its strongest scholarly explanation points to a Mozarabic-Arabic tree-name, while the Arabic hill theory remains a significant local interpretation.

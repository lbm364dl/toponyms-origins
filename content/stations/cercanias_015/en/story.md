Puente Alcocer station takes its name from the local place-name around it in Villaverde. The transport records are consistent: Renfe lists the station as Puente Alcocer on line C-5, with the address Calle Alcocer 54, while the Madrid transport authority gives the station access and interchange information under the same name.

The immediate name is therefore local and urban: a bridge or bridge-area known as Puente Alcocer, attached to Calle Alcocer and the surrounding road junctions of Villaverde. Madrid's official street list records Calle de Alcocer in the district of Villaverde, giving the street a firm municipal basis even if the station itself is the railway name rather than a street-name entry.

The older word behind Alcocer is much deeper than the modern station. Toponomasticon Hispaniae explains Andalusi qasar, from classical Arabic qasr, as 'palace, alcazar' and notes that the diminutive qusayr meant a small alcazar or alcazarejo; it also lists Alcocer among the cognate Hispanic toponyms. That makes the linguistic part of the traditional explanation strong, though it speaks to the toponym Alcocer in general rather than proving a medieval fortress at this exact point in Villaverde.

The station belongs to the late-20th-century reshaping of Cercanias Madrid. Contemporary press reported that the Atocha-Villaverde Alto section entered service on 24 September 1989, with four new stations: Puente Alcocer, Orcasitas, Doce de Octubre and Mendez Alvaro. The same reporting noted that the works were still unfinished when passengers first used the line, an unusually concrete snapshot of the station's first days.

For public wording, the safest reading is layered: the station is named after a local Puente Alcocer place-name, that local name is tied to Calle Alcocer, and Alcocer itself belongs to an Arabic-derived Iberian place-name family. The attractive idea of a small Islamic fort or watchtower at the bridge should be treated as possible background imagery, not as a verified fact for this station.

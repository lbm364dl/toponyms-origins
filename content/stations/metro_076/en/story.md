Hospital 12 de Octubre station takes its name from the hospital it serves. The Metro stop is on Line 3, at Glorieta de Málaga and Avenida de Córdoba, beside one of the major public hospital campuses in southern Madrid. The Community of Madrid's project page for the Line 3 extension describes the station as located next to Hospital 12 de Octubre, and the regional transport consortium lists it under that name with Line 3 services.

The name behind the station is older than the Metro stop. The hospital opened on 2 October 1973 with the name Ciudad Sanitaria 1º de Octubre. That original name pointed to 1 October, the date associated with Francisco Franco's assumption of power in the rebel zone in 1936, so the hospital's first public identity carried a clear Franco-era commemorative charge.

The hospital later became 12 de Octubre. The hospital's own official history says the change happened at the end of the 1980s, while other secondary accounts give 12 October 1988 as the exact date. I could not verify that exact day in an official renaming act, and there is an important wrinkle: El País published letters in late 1981 and early 1982 using the form Ciudad Sanitaria 12 de Octubre, while one of those same items also preserved the older '1º de Octubre' wording. The safest public wording is therefore that the hospital changed from the Francoist 1 October name to 12 de Octubre by the late 1980s, with earlier mixed usage already visible.

The new date had a different public meaning. Spain's Law 18/1987 established 12 October as the Fiesta Nacional de España, a national commemoration linked in the law to Spain's historical projection beyond Europe. In everyday language it is often associated with the Día de la Hispanidad and with Columbus's arrival in the Americas in 1492, but the official legal title is Fiesta Nacional de España.

The Metro station itself is much more recent. It opened on 21 April 2007 as part of the Line 3 extension from Legazpi to Villaverde Alto, designed to serve dense southern districts along Avenida de Córdoba and Avenida de Andalucía. For passengers, the name is practical wayfinding; historically, it also preserves the hospital's passage from a Franco-era institutional name to a democratic-period national-date name.

Lavapiés station is named for Plaza de Lavapiés and the surrounding historic area, one of Madrid's best-known popular place names even though it belongs administratively to the Embajadores neighborhood. Metro de Madrid built the station under this square and its adjoining streets, so the transport name is not a dedication created by the Metro: it is the older urban toponym carried underground.

The most credible origin of the word is the simplest one: *lava-pies*, “wash feet.” In early Madrid this was not a poetic metaphor for a flat plaza with a fountain, but a description of a difficult landscape. The streets around Jesús y María, Lavapiés, Olivar and Ave María descended through a steep, irregular area; when storms came, water rushed down the unpaved ground in torrents, turning the ravine and its streets into mud and soaking pedestrians' feet.

Documentary work pushes the name back at least to the fifteenth century. A 2019 note by José Manuel Castellanos Oñate, citing the study *Lavapiés, barranco y arrabal. Paisaje urbano al sur de Madrid, 1441-1547*, explains that early manuscript forms such as *alavapies* and *delavapies* can be read either as “a Lavapiés/de Lavapiés” or as “al Avapiés/del Avapiés,” because late-medieval script did not separate words or use capitalization consistently. That means the oldest spellings are evidence for the place name, but not a clean proof that *Avapiés* came first.

The famous Jewish-fountain explanation is much weaker. Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century writers popularized the idea of a Jewish quarter in Lavapiés and a fountain used for ritual washing before entering a synagogue. Modern studies of Madrid's medieval Jewish population and archaeological proposals for the Jewish quarter point instead to areas inside or beside the old walled town, especially around Santa María de la Almudena, the Campo del Rey and the Armería site, while describing medieval Lavapiés as a sparsely occupied or practically uninhabited arrabal.

The station itself belongs to a later chapter. The Community of Madrid records the first Line 3 section, Embajadores-Lavapiés-Sol, as inaugurated on 9 August 1936, while a Metro museum sheet gives 8 August 1936 for the station's line table and notes that the opening passed almost unnoticed because the Civil War had begun weeks earlier. The name has therefore been in railway use since the station opened, but the explanation of the ancient toponym remains probable rather than absolutely proven: the topographical reading is best supported, while the Jewish and inn stories survive as urban legend.

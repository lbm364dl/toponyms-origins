Alcalá de Henares station takes its name from the city of Alcalá de Henares, east of Madrid, in the Henares corridor. For passengers, the name is therefore a municipal place-name rather than a dedication to a person, event, or building. Adif lists the station as Alcalá de Henares, and Renfe's Madrid Cercanías network serves it on the C-2, C-7, and C-8 corridor services.

The first part of the name, Alcalá, is the key etymological element. Toponomasticon Hispaniae explains Andalusi Arabic qaláʿa, classical Arabic qalʿa, as 'fortress' or 'castle', and identifies it as the origin of many Alcalá place-names. In the local case, tourism and heritage sources connect the name with the Hispano-Muslim fortress Qalʿat Abd al-Salam, built on the opposite bank of the Henares in the ninth century as part of the defensive system of the Middle March.

The city, however, is older than its Arabic name. The Roman city was Complutum, located by the Henares River and later remembered in the adjective complutense. Municipal heritage material describes Complutum as one of the most important Roman cities of inland Hispania and the only Roman city of that scale in the present Community of Madrid. After the Islamic and medieval phases, the name Alcalá remained attached to the settlement even as population and urban life shifted back toward the plain.

The second element, Henares, anchors the name in the river landscape. The strongest etymological source found, Toponomasticon Hispaniae, derives Henares from the plural of henar, meaning a place with hay, ultimately connected with Latin fēnu. That is more precise than the common but weaker explanation that links Henares to Arabic nahr, 'river'. The river name also gives a second name to other towns in Guadalajara and Madrid, so it functions as a regional identifier.

For the station name, the safest reading is straightforward: it names the city, whose own name means, in broad terms, the castle or fortress associated with the Henares. The Arabic origin of Alcalá is very well supported; the river-name explanation is also well supported when treated as 'hayfields' rather than Arabic. Former station names were not found in authoritative sources, and the station appears to have used the municipal name from the railway's nineteenth-century origins.
